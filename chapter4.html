The Momentum Equation

Continuity counted the mass, the Bernoulli equation counted the energy. Neither can tell you what holds a pipe bend down. For that you need the third conservation law — of momentum — and it is the one that turns fluid mechanics into structural design.

4.1 What Momentum Adds

You now have two equations for a control volume. Continuity (§3.4) says mass is conserved; the Bernoulli equation (§3.5) says energy is conserved. Between them they will size a pipe and read a meter. But ask either of them what force the water exerts on the bend it is being pushed round, and neither has anything to say — because neither contains a force.

Momentum is the missing law, and the reason is Newton’s: a force is what changes momentum. If a fluid enters a control volume travelling one way and leaves travelling another, its momentum has changed, so something must have pushed it. That something is the pipe — and by Newton’s third law the fluid pushes back on the pipe just as hard.

The division of labour

In almost every problem in this chapter you will use all three, in this order: continuity to get the velocities, the Bernoulli equation to get the pressures, and momentum to get the force. Trying to start with momentum is the commonest way to get stuck, because the momentum equation needs velocities and pressures as inputs — it does not produce them.

One more difference is worth stating now. Continuity and the Bernoulli equation are scalar equations: one equation, one number. Momentum is a vector equation: it has a direction, and in two dimensions it is really two equations. Almost every mistake made in this chapter is a sign mistake, and almost every sign mistake comes from forgetting that.

4.2 Newton’s Second Law for a Flowing Fluid

Newton’s second law is usually met as F = ma, but the form he actually wrote is more general and is the one we need:

the rate of change of momentum of a body is equal to the resultant force acting on the body, and takes place in the direction of that force

Written as an equation, with momentum being mass times velocity:

F = d(mu)dt (4.1)

Applying it to a streamtube

Take the streamtube of Figure 4.1 and consider a small interval δt. The mass entering in that interval is ρ1A1u1δt, and it carries momentum equal to that mass times its velocity. Since the flow is steady, an equal mass must leave at section 2 over the same interval. The fluid in between has not changed at all, so it contributes nothing.

(4.2) momentum entering = ρ1A1u1δt · u1 momentum leaving = ρ2A2u2δt · u2 and by continuity, both masses are the same: ρ1A1u1 = ρ2A2u2 = ṁ

Newton’s law says the force equals the rate of change of momentum, so divide the change by δt:

(4.3) F = ṁδt(u2 − u1)δt = ṁ(u2 − u1) and since ṁ = ρQ, F = ρQ(u2 − u1) = ρA1u1(u2 − u1)
The momentum equation, in words

Force = mass flow rate × change in velocity, with the change taken as out minus in. Note what is not in it: no length, no volume, no shape of the pipe between the two sections, no friction coefficient. Only what crosses the two faces matters. That is why the momentum equation is so powerful — and why it tells you nothing about what happened inside.

Which force is this?

F in equation (4.3) is the total force on the fluid, from every source: the pipe walls, the pressure at the two faces, and gravity. Pulling out the one piece an engineer actually wants — the force between the fluid and the pipe — is the whole business of §4.4. Do not skip it and do not guess.

4.3 Momentum in Two Dimensions

Equation (4.3) was written as though the flow ran along a single straight line. It rarely does. The fluid in Figure 4.1 enters at one angle and leaves at another, and a force has a direction as well as a size.

The fix is the standard one from statics: choose a pair of axes, resolve every velocity onto them, and write the momentum equation separately for each axis. Momentum in the x direction can only be changed by a force in the x direction, so the two equations do not interfere with each other.

Resolving the velocities in Figure 4.2 and applying equation (4.3) to each axis in turn:

(4.4) Fx = ṁ(u2 cosθ2 − u1 cosθ1) = ρQ(u2x − u1x)
(4.5) Fy = ṁ(u2 sinθ2 − u1 sinθ1) = ρQ(u2y − u1y)

These two components are then recombined into a single force by the usual rules:

F = Fx2 + Fy2 (4.6)
φ = tan−1FyFx (4.7)

where φ is measured from the x axis in the same sense as the angles θ.

The three sign traps
  • Out minus in, never in minus out. The change of anything across a control volume is always what left less what entered.
  • A velocity pointing backwards along an axis is negative. A jet that reverses through 180° has u2x = −u2, so the change is −u2 − u1 and the force is nearly twice what a plate that simply stops the jet would feel. This is not a subtlety; it is the whole reason a Pelton bucket is shaped the way it is.
  • A negative answer is an answer. It means the force acts the other way along the axis you chose. Do not silently make it positive.

4.4 The Four Forces

Equation (4.3) gives the total force acting on the fluid in the control volume. That is not usually what you want to know. What a designer needs is the force between the fluid and the solid boundary — the thing that has to be bolted down. So the total must be broken into its parts.

There are three sources of force on the fluid, and they add:

FT = FR + FP + FB (4.8)
FT — total force N
The whole force on the fluid, and the one equation (4.3) computes: FT = ρQ(u2 − u1). Never measured — always calculated from the velocities.
FP — pressure force N
The force of the fluid pressure acting on the inlet and outlet faces of the control volume, from the fluid outside it. Always pA, always pushing into the control volume, always normal to the face. Chapter 2 §2.6.
FB — body force N
The weight of the fluid in the control volume, acting vertically downwards. For a horizontal bend it is zero in both working directions; for a short nozzle it is usually small enough to neglect. Say which you are doing.
FR — reaction force N
The force of the solid boundary on the fluid. This is the unknown you are solving for.

Rearranging equation (4.8) gives the working form of the whole chapter:

FR = FT − FP − FB (4.9)
The last step, and the one most often forgotten

FR is the force the pipe exerts on the fluid. The force the fluid exerts on the pipe — the one the thrust block must resist, the one you quote in a report — is equal and opposite, by Newton’s third law:

Fon pipe = −FR

An answer that stops at FR and calls it the force on the bend has the direction exactly backwards.

4.5 A Procedure That Always Works

Every problem in §4.6 to §4.9 is the same problem with different geometry. Learn the sequence once and the rest is arithmetic.

The seven steps
  1. Draw the control volume and mark the inlet and outlet faces. Cut them where you know, or can find, the velocity and the pressure — in practice, where the streamlines are parallel (§3.9).
  2. Choose the axes and mark the positive direction of each. Put x along the inlet flow unless there is a good reason not to; it removes one angle from the arithmetic.
  3. Find the velocities from continuity: Q = A1u1 = A2u2.
  4. Find the pressures from the Bernoulli equation, if they are not given.
  5. Compute FT on each axis, from equations (4.4) and (4.5).
  6. Compute FP on each axis, and FB if it matters. Then FR = FT − FP − FB.
  7. Combine and reverse. Get the magnitude and angle from equations (4.6) and (4.7), then reverse the sign to give the force on the solid boundary.
Where to cut the control volume

The single most useful skill in this chapter is choosing the faces. Cut them where the streamlines are parallel, so the pressure is uniform across the face and FP = pA is exactly right. Cut them where the velocity is known or findable. And cut them so the boundary you care about lies inside the volume — if the bend is not inside, its reaction never appears in the equations.

4.6 Force on a Nozzle

Start with the simplest case, where everything happens along one axis. A nozzle at the end of a hose accelerates the water by reducing the area. The flow never changes direction, so there is no y equation at all.

Working through the seven steps

Steps 3 and 4. Continuity gives both velocities from the discharge, and the Bernoulli equation gives the inlet pressure, since the outlet is at atmospheric:

(4.10) u1 = QA1 and u2 = QA2 p1 = ρ2u22 − u12 from the Bernoulli equation, with p2 = 0 gauge

Step 5. The total force, with both velocities along x so θ1 = θ2 = 0:

FTx = ρQ(u2 − u1) (4.11)

Step 6. The pressure force. Both faces are normal to x. The inlet pressure pushes the fluid forwards, in the +x direction; the outlet pressure would push it backwards, but it is zero gauge:

FPx = p1A1 − p2A2 = p1A1 (4.12)

The nozzle is horizontal, so the body force has no x component: FBx = 0. Then from equation (4.9):

FRx = ρQ(u2 − u1) − p1A1 (4.13)
Reading the answer

FRx comes out negative: the nozzle pulls the fluid backwards, because it is holding back water that the pressure behind is trying to push out. Reversing the sign, the water pushes the nozzle forwards, away from the hose — which is exactly the force you feel trying to tear a fire hose out of your hands, and the force the coupling threads have to carry.

4.7 Force on a Pipe Bend

Now the flow changes direction, and both components are live. This is the case that matters most in practice: every buried irrigation main has bends, and every bend needs a thrust block sized from this calculation.

Take x along the inlet, so θ1 = 0 and θ2 = θ. Then:

(4.14) FTx = ρQ(u2 cosθ − u1) FTy = ρQ(u2 sinθ − 0) = ρQu2 sinθ
(4.15) FPx = p1A1 − p2A2 cosθ FPy = 0 − p2A2 sinθ

For a bend in a horizontal plane the weight acts perpendicular to both working axes, so FBx = FBy = 0. Subtracting as equation (4.9) requires:

(4.16) FRx = ρQ(u2 cosθ − u1) − p1A1 + p2A2 cosθ FRy = ρQu2 sinθ + p2A2 sinθ

and the force on the bend itself is the reverse of the resultant of these two:

Fon bend = FRx2 + FRy2 directed opposite to φ = tan−1FRyFRx (4.17)
Which term dominates?

In a real pipeline the pressure terms are usually far larger than the momentum terms. For a 300 mm main at 4 bar carrying 1.5 m/s, pA is about 28 kN while ρQu is about 160 N — a factor of nearly 200. This is why a thrust block is sized almost entirely by the static pressure, and why a pipeline is at its most vulnerable during a pressure surge rather than at maximum flow. But do not drop the momentum term without checking: in a high-velocity, low-pressure jet the balance reverses completely.

A 180° bend is not twice a 90° bend

Put θ = 180° into equation (4.16): cos 180° = −1 and sin 180° = 0, so FRy vanishes and FRx = −ρQ(u2 + u1) − p1A1 − p2A2. Every term now adds. A U-bend carries roughly 1.4 times the thrust of a right-angle bend of the same size and pressure, all of it along one line.

4.8 Impact of a Jet on a Plane Surface

A free jet is the easiest case of all, because it is surrounded by atmosphere. The pressure is atmospheric everywhere on the control surface, so FP = 0 in gauge terms and equation (4.9) collapses to FR = FT. There is nothing to subtract.

A jet striking a plate at right angles

Take x along the jet. The water arrives at u1 = u and leaves sideways along the plate, so its x velocity afterwards is zero. All of the x momentum is destroyed:

(4.18) FTx = ρQ(0 − u) = −ρQu and since Q = Au, the force on the plate is F = ρQu = ρAu2

Note the square. Doubling the jet velocity gives four times the force: twice the mass arriving each second, each kilogram carrying twice the momentum.

A jet striking an inclined plate

If the plate is set at θ to the jet, only the component of the jet velocity normal to the plate is destroyed; the component along the plate simply runs off. Resolving normal to the plate:

Fn = ρQu sinθ = ρAu2 sinθ (4.19)

At θ = 90° this recovers equation (4.18); at θ = 0 the jet grazes the plate and exerts no normal force at all, which is why a deflector plate is always angled to the flow rather than set square to it.

A jet striking a moving plate

If the plate retreats at velocity v along the jet, sit on the plate and watch. The water now appears to approach at the relative velocity u − v, and the mass arriving each second falls too, because the jet has to catch up:

(4.20) ṁrel = ρA(u − v) F = ρA(u − v) · (u − v) = ρA(u − v)2
Force is not the point — power is

A stationary plate feels the biggest force but does no work, because it does not move. A plate running away at the jet speed feels no force at all. Somewhere in between lies the maximum power, P = Fv, and finding it is the whole design problem of a water turbine. §4.9 solves it.

4.9 Curved Vanes and the Pelton Wheel

A flat plate throws the water sideways, and sideways momentum is wasted. A curved vane turns the water right round and sends it back the way it came — and by the sign rule of §4.3, a reversal changes the momentum by nearly twice as much as a stop does.

The general curved vane

Let the jet be turned through an angle β measured from its original direction, and assume no friction, so the speed is unchanged: u2 = u1 = u. Taking x along the incoming jet and using equation (4.4):

(4.21) FTx = ρQ(u cosβ − u) = −ρQu1 − cosβ so the force on the vane is F = ρQu1 − cosβ
Table 4.2. The factor (1 − cos β) for the shapes met in practice. Everything in §4.8 and §4.9 is one equation with one number changed.
ShapeTurned through β1 − cos βForce on a stationary vane
Flat plate, jet grazing0°00
Flat plate, normal to the jet90°1ρAu2
Practical Pelton bucket165°1.971.97 ρAu2
Ideal hemispherical cup180°22 ρAu2

Turning the jet right round therefore doubles the force compared with simply stopping it, for the same jet. That factor of two, available for nothing but a change of shape, is the reason every impulse turbine ever built uses a cup rather than a paddle.

The Pelton wheel

Now let the buckets move. A Pelton wheel carries many buckets round a rim at velocity v, and as one bucket leaves the jet the next enters it. The consequence is important and easily missed: the whole jet is used. The mass flow rate delivered to the wheel is ρAu, set by the nozzle, not ρA(u − v).

But the velocity change each bucket imposes is still governed by the relative velocity u − v. Combining the two:

F = ρAu(u − v)1 − cosβ (4.22)
P = Fv = ρAu(u − v)1 − cosβv (4.23)

To find the wheel speed that gives the most power, differentiate with respect to v and set the result to zero. Only the product v(u − v) depends on v:

(4.24) dPdv = ρAu1 − cosβu − 2v = 0 v = u2 — the buckets should run at half the jet speed
Why one third, and why one half

Repeat the same differentiation for a single plate or bucket, where the mass flow is ρA(u − v) as in equation (4.20), and the answer comes out v = u/3 instead. The two results differ because of what the mass flow rate is, not because of any difference in the momentum argument:

  • One bucket — the jet must chase it, so less water arrives each second as the bucket speeds up. Best at v = u/3.
  • A wheel of buckets — the next bucket is always arriving, so all the water is caught whatever the wheel speed. Best at v = u/2.

A real Pelton wheel runs a little below u/2, at about 0.46 u, because friction in the bucket makes the return speed slightly less than the arrival speed.

Substituting v = u/2 and β = 180° into equation (4.23) gives the ideal maximum power:

(4.25) Pmax = ρAu · u2 · 1 − cos 180° · u2 = 12ρAu3 and the power carried by the jet is 12ṁu2 = 12ρAu3 — identical, so the ideal efficiency is 100 %

An ideal Pelton wheel extracts all the kinetic energy of the jet: the water is left at rest in the ground frame, having given up everything it had. Real machines reach 90–92 %, which is the highest efficiency of any prime mover in common use.

4.10 Beyond the Core — Where the Thrust Goes

Extension material — not examinable

This section goes beyond CIVE 1400 §3.6–3.7. It is here because §4.7 produces a number in kilonewtons and stops, and the obvious next question — what holds that back? — is answered on every irrigation scheme you will ever work on. Nothing here is examined. Any example or problem drawing on it is marked §4.10.

Thrust blocks

The force you computed in §4.7 has to go somewhere. In a pipeline with welded or flanged joints it can travel along the pipe wall as tension, but the pipe used on an irrigation scheme — PVC or ductile iron with push-fit spigot-and-socket joints — carries almost no tension across a joint. The thrust must therefore be taken into the ground by a thrust block: a mass of concrete cast between the fitting and undisturbed soil.

Thrust blocks are needed wherever the momentum or pressure balance is broken:

  • Bends — the case of §4.7, resultant pointing outward along the bisector of the turn.
  • Tees — thrust along the branch, equal to pA of the branch.
  • Reducers — unbalanced pA from the difference in area, as in §4.6.
  • Dead ends and caps — the full pA, and the largest thrust of all for a given diameter.
  • Valves — a closed valve is a dead end, so a line valve needs blocks on both sides.

The block is sized so that the soil bearing pressure stays within an allowable value:

Ablock ≥ Fthrustσallow (4.26)

with σallow typically 50–100 kPa for soft clay and 200–400 kPa for dense sand or gravel. The two rules that matter in the field are that the block must bear against undisturbed ground, not backfill, and that the design pressure is the test pressure or the surge pressure, not the working pressure.

Where the energy went: the sudden expansion

Chapter 3 said the Bernoulli equation cannot account for losses. The momentum equation can find one of them exactly, and the trick is worth seeing: apply momentum and energy to the same control volume and subtract.

At an abrupt enlargement from A1 to A2, the jet cannot follow the sudden step, so it separates and mixes turbulently. Momentum, which does not care about the mess inside, still holds; energy does not. Eliminating the pressures between the two gives the Borda–Carnot loss:

hL = u1 − u222g (4.27)

This is the only major head loss in the whole course that can be derived from first principles rather than measured. Every other loss coefficient in Chapter 5 comes from a laboratory. Note the limiting case: a pipe discharging into a large reservoir has u2 ≈ 0, so the whole velocity head u12/2g is lost — which is why the exit loss coefficient in a pipe-network calculation is 1.0.

The hydraulic jump

Water leaving under a sluice gate, or over a drop structure, arrives fast and shallow. It cannot stay that way, and it returns to a slow, deep state through a hydraulic jump — a short, violent standing wave that dissipates a great deal of energy in a few metres.

The jump is turbulent and lossy, so the Bernoulli equation is useless across it. Momentum is not. Applying equation (4.9) to a control volume enclosing the jump in a rectangular channel, with the hydrostatic pressure forces of §2.6 on the two faces, gives the sequent depth relation:

(4.28) y2y1 = 12 1 + 8Fr12 − 1 where the Froude number is Fr1 = u1gy1

A jump forms only if Fr1 > 1 — that is, only if the incoming flow is supercritical, running faster than a surface wave can travel upstream. This single criterion governs the design of every stilling basin below every gate and every drop on an irrigation system, and you will meet it properly in year two.

The rotating sprinkler

An impact sprinkler turns because the jet leaving its nozzle carries momentum away tangentially, and the reaction spins the head. The same argument applied about an axis rather than along a line gives the moment of momentum, or angular momentum, equation:

T = ρQ(r2ut2 − r1ut1) (4.29)

where ut is the tangential velocity component and r the radius at which it acts. This is the equation behind rotary sprinklers, centrifugal pump impellers and Francis turbines alike — three machines that look nothing like one another and obey the same line of algebra.

Water hammer, seen as momentum

Chapter 1 §1.11 introduced water hammer through the bulk modulus. Momentum gives the same result more directly: shutting a valve destroys the momentum of a whole column of moving water, and the faster you destroy it, the larger the force. For an instantaneous closure the pressure rise is:

Δp = ρc Δu (4.30)

where c is the speed of the pressure wave, about 1200 m/s in a rigid steel pipe and 300–500 m/s in PVC. For water at 1.5 m/s in PVC, Δp ≈ 1000 × 400 × 1.5 = 600 kPa — six bar added to the working pressure by nothing more than closing a valve quickly. That is the number behind every instruction to close an irrigation valve slowly, and behind the surge pressure used to size the thrust blocks above.

4.11 Worked Examples

Every one of these follows the seven steps of §4.5 in order. Throughout, ρ = 1000 kg m−3, g = 9.81 m s−2, all pressures are gauge, and losses are neglected unless stated.

Example 4.1 — The force on a hose nozzle

A fire hose of 75 mm bore ends in a nozzle of 25 mm bore and discharges 15 l s−1 to atmosphere. Find the force the water exerts on the nozzle, and say which way it acts.

Steps 1–2. Control volume from the hose inlet to a section cut in the free jet, just clear of the nozzle where the pressure is atmospheric. One axis, x, along the flow.

Step 3 — continuity. A1 = 4.418 × 10−3 m2, A2 = 4.909 × 10−4 m2.
u1 = 0.015 / 4.418 × 10−3 = 3.40 m s−1; u2 = 0.015 / 4.909 × 10−4 = 30.56 m s−1

Step 4 — the Bernoulli equation, horizontal, with p2 = 0:
p1 = ½ρ(u22 − u12) = 500(933.8 − 11.5) = 461.1 kPa

Step 5 — total force. FTx = ρQ(u2 − u1) = 1000 × 0.015 × (30.56 − 3.40) = 407 N

Step 6 — pressure and body forces. FPx = p1A1 = 461 100 × 4.418 × 10−3 = 2037 N; FBx = 0 (horizontal).
FRx = 407 − 2037 = −1630 N

Step 7. The nozzle pulls the water back with 1630 N, so the water pushes the nozzle forwards with 1.63 kN, away from the hose — about the weight of a small car, which is why a fire hose needs two people. Notice that the pressure term is five times the momentum term even here.

Example 4.2 — A right-angle bend at constant diameter

A 300 mm pipe carries 0.25 m3 s−1 round a 90° bend lying in a horizontal plane. The gauge pressure is 400 kPa and may be taken as the same at both ends. Find the magnitude and direction of the force on the bend.

Steps 3–4. A = π(0.300)2/4 = 0.07069 m2, the same at both faces, so u1 = u2 = 0.25/0.07069 = 3.54 m s−1, and p1 = p2 = 400 kPa.

Step 5. With x along the inlet and θ = 90°, so cos θ = 0 and sin θ = 1:
FTx = 1000 × 0.25 × (0 − 3.54) = −884 N
FTy = 1000 × 0.25 × (3.54 − 0) = +884 N

Step 6. pA = 400 000 × 0.07069 = 28 274 N, so
FPx = 28 274 − 0 = +28 274 N; FPy = 0 − 28 274 = −28 274 N
FRx = −884 − 28 274 = −29 158 N
FRy = 884 + 28 274 = +29 158 N

Step 7. F = √(29 1582 + 29 1582) = 41.2 kN, and the reaction on the fluid points at 135°, so the force on the bend acts at −45° — outward along the bisector of the turn, exactly where you would put the thrust block.
The momentum terms contributed 884 N out of 29 158, about 3 %. The pressure did the rest.

Example 4.3 — A jet striking a plate

A jet 30 mm in diameter strikes a large flat plate at 25 m s−1. Find the force on the plate (a) when the plate is normal to the jet and (b) when it is inclined at 40° to the jet.

A = π(0.030)2/4 = 7.069 × 10−4 m2, so Q = Au = 7.069 × 10−4 × 25 = 0.01767 m3 s−1.

The jet is free, so the pressure is atmospheric all round the control surface and FP = 0. There is nothing to subtract.

(a) F = ρAu2 = 1000 × 7.069 × 10−4 × 252 = 442 N

(b) Fn = ρAu2 sin 40° = 442 × 0.643 = 284 N, normal to the plate.
Tilting the plate by 50° from square has shed 36 % of the load — the reason a deflector or a splash plate is always set at an angle.

Example 4.4 — A Pelton wheel

A Pelton wheel is driven by a 50 mm jet at 40 m s−1. The buckets turn the jet through 165°. Find (a) the force on a single stationary bucket, (b) the force and power when the wheel runs at half the jet speed, and (c) the efficiency at that speed.

A = π(0.050)2/4 = 1.963 × 10−3 m2, Q = 1.963 × 10−3 × 40 = 0.0785 m3 s−1.
1 − cos 165° = 1 − (−0.966) = 1.966

(a) stationary. F = ρQu(1 − cos β) = 1000 × 0.0785 × 40 × 1.966 = 6.18 kN
Against a flat plate the same jet would give 3.14 kN. The cup shape is worth a factor of 1.97.

(b) running at v = u/2 = 20 m s−1. The whole jet is still caught, because the next bucket is always arriving, so use equation (4.22):
F = ρAu(u − v)(1 − cos β) = 1000 × 1.963 × 10−3 × 40 × 20 × 1.966 = 3.09 kN
P = Fv = 3088 × 20 = 61.8 kW

(c) The power in the jet is ½ρAu3 = 0.5 × 1000 × 1.963 × 10−3 × 403 = 62.8 kW.
Efficiency = 61.8 / 62.8 = 98.3 %
The whole of the 1.7 % shortfall is the 15° the bucket falls short of a full reversal. A real wheel loses another 6–8 % to friction and windage.

Example 4.5 — A tapering bend: all seven steps

A horizontal bend turns the flow through 45° while reducing from 200 mm to 150 mm diameter. It carries 0.08 m3 s−1 and the inlet gauge pressure is 250 kPa. Find the force on the bend.

Step 3 — continuity. A1 = 0.03142 m2, A2 = 0.01767 m2; u1 = 2.55 m s−1, u2 = 4.53 m s−1.

Step 4 — the Bernoulli equation, horizontal:
p2 = p1 + ½ρ(u12 − u22) = 250 000 + 500(6.48 − 20.49) = 243.0 kPa
p1A1 = 7854 N and p2A2 = 4294 N.

Step 5 — total force, x along the inlet, θ = 45°:
FTx = 1000 × 0.08 × (4.53 × 0.7071 − 2.55) = 80 × 0.655 = +52 N
FTy = 80 × (4.53 × 0.7071) = +256 N

Step 6 — pressure force, both acting into the control volume:
FPx = 7854 − 4294 × 0.7071 = 7854 − 3036 = +4818 N
FPy = 0 − 4294 × 0.7071 = −3036 N
Horizontal, so FB has no component on either axis. Then
FRx = 52 − 4818 = −4765 N; FRy = 256 + 3036 = +3293 N

Step 7. F = √(47652 + 32932) = 5.79 kN
φ = tan−1(3293 / −4765) = 145.4° on the fluid, so the force on the bend acts at 34.6° below the inlet direction — outward from the turn, as it must be.

Example 4.6 — Sizing a thrust block §4.10

A 250 mm PVC irrigation main ends in a capped dead end. The working pressure is 600 kPa and the line is tested at 1.5 times that. The soil at the site has an allowable bearing pressure of 120 kPa. Find the thrust on the cap and the bearing area the thrust block needs.

The design pressure. A thrust block is sized on the worst case the pipe will ever see, which is the test pressure, not the working pressure: p = 1.5 × 600 = 900 kPa.

The thrust. A dead end is the simplest momentum problem there is: the velocity is zero on both sides, so FT = 0 and the whole thrust is pressure.
A = π(0.250)2/4 = 0.04909 m2
F = pA = 900 000 × 0.04909 = 44.2 kN

The block. From equation (4.26), Ablock ≥ 44 200 / 120 000 = 0.368 m2.

A bearing face of about 0.65 m × 0.60 m gives 0.39 m2 and will do — provided it bears against undisturbed ground and not against backfill.
Compare this with the 41.2 kN of the 300 mm bend in Example 4.2: a plain cap on a smaller pipe produces more thrust than a right-angle bend on a larger one, because a dead end turns the flow through a full 180° in effect and wastes none of the pressure force.

c — speed of a pressure wave in a pipe m s−1
FT — total force on the fluid N
FP — pressure force on the control-volume faces N
FB — body force, the weight of the fluid N
FR — reaction of the boundary on the fluid N
Fn — force normal to a plate N
Fr — Froude number, u/√(gy) –
g — gravitational acceleration, 9.81 m s−2
hL — head lost m
ṁ — mass flow rate, ρQ kg s−1
p — pressure, gauge unless stated Pa
P — power W
Q — discharge m3 s−1
r — radius at which a tangential velocity acts m
T — torque N m
u — fluid velocity, mean over the section m s−1
ut — tangential component of velocity m s−1
v — velocity of a moving plate, vane or bucket m s−1
y — depth of flow in a channel m
β — angle a jet is turned through by a vane degrees
θ — angle of a velocity to the x axis degrees
φ — angle of the resultant force to the x axis degrees
ρ — density kg m−3
σallow — allowable soil bearing pressure Pa

Work through these before the problems in §4.14. Four of the questions come from the extension material in §4.10 and are marked as such.

4.14 Problems

Take g = 9.81 m/s2 and ρwater = 1000 kg/m3. All pressures are gauge and all bends lie in a horizontal plane unless stated. Neglect friction losses. Work through the seven steps of §4.5 every time; the marks are in the working, and in particular in getting the signs and the final reversal right. Problems marked §4.10 draw on the extension material and are not examinable.

  1. A nozzle reduces from 60 mm to 20 mm bore and discharges 8 l/s to atmosphere. Find the two velocities, the inlet gauge pressure, and the magnitude and direction of the force on the nozzle. 2.83 and 25.5 m/s; 320 kPa; 724 N pushing the nozzle away from the pipe
  2. A 200 mm pipe carries 50 l/s round a 90° bend at a gauge pressure of 300 kPa, which may be taken as the same at both ends. Find the magnitude and direction of the force on the bend. 13.4 kN acting at 45° outward from the bisector of the turn
  3. Repeat Problem 2 for a 45° bend, everything else unchanged. Compare the two answers and explain the ratio you find. 7.27 kN at 67.5° to the inlet. The ratio is 0.54, which is sin 22.5°/sin 45° — the thrust on a bend of equal area goes as 2pA sin(θ/2)
  4. A jet 25 mm in diameter travelling at 30 m/s strikes a large flat plate. Find the force (a) with the plate normal to the jet, (b) with the plate inclined at 30° to the jet, and (c) with the plate normal to the jet but retreating along it at 12 m/s. (a) 442 N (b) 221 N (c) 159 N
  5. A jet 40 mm in diameter at 20 m/s is deflected through 150° by a stationary curved vane. Find the force on the vane, and state what fraction of it a flat plate would have caught. 938 N; a flat plate would give 503 N, so 54 % — the curve is worth a factor of 1.87
  6. A Pelton wheel is driven by a 60 mm jet at 35 m/s. The buckets deflect the jet through 170° and the wheel runs at 16 m/s. Find the force on the buckets, the power developed and the efficiency. Is the wheel running at its best speed? 3.73 kN; 59.7 kW; 98.5 %. Best speed is u/2 = 17.5 m/s, which would give 60.2 kW at 99.2 %
  7. A horizontal bend turns the flow through 30° while reducing from 250 mm to 200 mm diameter. It carries 0.12 m3/s at an inlet gauge pressure of 180 kPa. Find the force on the bend. p2 = 175.7 kPa; 4.96 kN acting at 37.1° below the inlet direction
  8. A 400 mm main is closed by a blank flange at a gauge pressure of 800 kPa. Find the thrust on the flange, and explain why a dead end produces more thrust than a 90° bend of the same diameter at the same pressure. 100.5 kN. A bend redirects the pressure force, so the two ends partly oppose one another and the resultant is 2pA sin 45° = 1.41pA; a dead end opposes nothing, so the full 2pA…1pA acts on one face alone with nothing to cancel it
  9. A jet 50 mm in diameter at 24 m/s strikes a single flat plate that is free to move along the jet. Show that the power delivered is greatest when the plate travels at u/3, find that power, and find the efficiency. Why is the answer u/3 here but u/2 for a Pelton wheel? 4.02 kW at v = 8 m/s; jet power 13.57 kW, so 29.6 % (the maximum for a single plate is 8/27). A single plate must be chased by the jet, so the mass flow it catches falls as it speeds up; on a wheel the next bucket always catches the whole jet
  10. §4.10 Water leaves a sluice gate at 4.5 m/s in a layer 0.30 m deep in a wide rectangular channel. Find the Froude number, say whether a hydraulic jump will form, and if so find the depth after it. Fr1 = 2.62, which is supercritical, so a jump forms; the sequent depth is 0.97 m
  11. §4.10 A 100 mm pipe carrying 25 l/s expands abruptly to 200 mm. Find the head lost at the expansion and the power it wastes. 0.291 m of water; 71 W lost continuously
  12. §4.10 A 200 mm PVC main carries water at 1.8 m/s under a working pressure of 500 kPa. A valve is closed suddenly; the pressure wave travels at 420 m/s. Find the surge pressure, the peak pressure, and the thrust on a 90° bend in the line at that peak. Compare it with the thrust at working pressure. Surge 756 kPa, peak 1256 kPa; thrust 55.8 kN against 22.2 kN — a factor of 2.5, which is why thrust blocks are sized on surge and valves are closed slowly
