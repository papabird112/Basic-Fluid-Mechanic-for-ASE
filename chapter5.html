5.1 Where the Ideal Fluid Runs Out

Chapter 1 §1.5 defined viscosity, measured it, and tabulated it. Then nothing in Chapters 2 to 4 used it. That was not an oversight: every one of those chapters bought its simplicity by assuming an ideal fluid — one with no viscosity, in which no shear stress can act and therefore nothing can resist the flow.

The price of that assumption comes due here. Put a row of piezometers along a straight, level pipe carrying water at a steady rate and you will find the water standing a little lower in each one than in the one before. Nothing in Chapter 3 permits that: with z constant and u constant, equation (3.11) insists the pressure head must be constant too.

The head that disappears is called the friction head loss, written hf. It has not vanished: it has been converted into heat by the shearing of one layer of fluid over another, and it is gone for good as far as the flow is concerned. Adding it to the Bernoulli equation gives the form used for the rest of your career:

p1ρg + u122g + z1 = p2ρg + u222g + z2 + hf (5.1)
What is left to find

Equation (5.1) is not an achievement; it is a promissory note. It says a loss exists and gives it a name, but it does not say how big it is. Everything from §5.2 to §5.9 is the work of turning hf into a number you can calculate from the things you actually know: the pipe, the fluid and the flow rate.

And there is a second, less obvious debt to settle. Chapter 3 §3.3 asserted, without justification, that the mean velocity in a pipe is half the maximum in one case and about 0.82 of it in another, and never said why there were two cases at all. By §5.7 you will be able to derive the first figure exactly and explain the second.

5.2 Reynolds’ Experiment

In 1883 Osborne Reynolds, at Manchester, ran what is probably the most consequential experiment in the history of fluid mechanics, and it is simple enough to repeat in an afternoon. Water flows from a tank through a straight glass tube, the rate controlled by a valve at the outlet. A fine filament of dye is injected on the axis at the inlet.

Why the dye matters

The dye is not decoration; it is the measurement. A dye filament that stays intact proves there is no transport across the flow — no fluid crossing from one layer to the next. The moment the filament disperses, fluid is being carried sideways in bulk. That sideways transport is what turbulence is, and it is why turbulent flow mixes, scours, and loses far more energy than laminar flow.

Reynolds then did the part that mattered. He repeated the experiment with tubes of different diameter, and with water at different temperatures — that is, at different viscosities — and found that the change did not happen at a fixed velocity. It happened at a fixed value of a particular combination of velocity, diameter, density and viscosity. That combination now carries his name.

5.3 The Reynolds Number

The combination Reynolds found is now written Re and called the Reynolds number:

(5.2) Re = ρudμ = udν where ν = μ/ρ is the kinematic viscosity of §1.10

Here u is the mean velocity and d the pipe diameter. The second form is the one to use in practice, because a single tabulated number — ν ≈ 1.0 × 10−6 m2 s−1 for water at 20 °C — replaces two.

It is a pure number

Apply the test of §1.3 and something remarkable happens:

Dimensions of the Reynolds number

[ρud/μ] = (ML−3)(LT−1)(L) ÷ (ML−1T−1) = M L−1 T−1 ÷ M L−1 T−1 = 1

Every dimension cancels. Re is dimensionless — it has no units, and it comes out the same whether you work in SI, in feet and pounds, or in any other consistent system. That is exactly why it can have a universal critical value, which no quantity carrying units could ever have.

What it means physically

The Reynolds number is best read as a ratio of two competing tendencies:

Re  =  inertial forces — the fluid’s tendency to keep going and to break upviscous forces — the fluid’s tendency to stick together in layers

When Re is small, viscosity wins: any small disturbance is damped out by the fluid’s internal friction before it can grow, and the layers stay intact. When Re is large, inertia wins: a disturbance is carried along and amplified faster than viscosity can smother it, and the flow breaks down into eddies.

The critical values, for flow in a circular pipe
Re < 2000  →  laminar   ·   2000 < Re < 4000  →  transitional   ·   Re > 4000  →  turbulent

These numbers belong to pipe flow and to nothing else. A flat plate changes over at Re near 5 × 105, an open channel at about 500 on its own definition. Quoting 2000 for anything but a pipe is a mistake, and quoting it without saying which length was used for d is meaningless.

What this means for an irrigation main

Take the pipe from the opening of this chapter: 150 mm bore, water at 1.5 m/s, 20 °C.

Re = 1.5 × 0.151.0 × 10−6 = 2.25 × 105

That is more than fifty times the turbulent threshold. To make the same pipe run laminar you would have to slow the water to u = 2000 × 10−6/0.15 = 0.013 m s−1 — thirteen millimetres a second, a discharge of about a quarter of a litre per second.

The conclusion to take away

Water in any pipe you will ever design is turbulent. Laminar flow is not a design case for a water main; it is a teaching case, and a real case only for small bores, slow flows or thick fluids. In agricultural engineering you will meet it in exactly three places: inside a drip-irrigation emitter, where the passage is a fraction of a millimetre wide; in groundwater seeping through soil; and in oil pipework, where ν can be a hundred times that of water.

So why spend §5.6 to §5.8 on it? Because laminar flow is the only case that can be solved exactly from first principles. It is the anchor everything else is measured against, and it is where the shear stress of Chapter 1 finally does visible work.

5.5 What Experiment Says About Friction

Before deriving anything, look at what the measurement actually gives. Reynolds, and many after him, measured hf over a fixed length of pipe at a series of velocities and plotted one against the other on logarithmic axes — which turns a power law hf ∝ un into a straight line of slope n.

Reading the two slopes off the plot gives the whole of the experimental result:

(5.3) hf ∝ u1 in laminar flow hf ∝ un, with n = 1.7 to 2.0 in turbulent flow
Why that exponent is the whole story

Everything expensive about pipelines follows from n ≈ 2. Double the flow through a given pipe and the friction loss quadruples; the pumping power, which is ρgQhf, goes up eight times. It is also why increasing the diameter is such a powerful remedy: at fixed discharge, u ∝ 1/d2, so — as §5.9 will show — hf falls as roughly 1/d5. Going from a 100 mm to a 150 mm main cuts the friction loss to about one eighth.

5.6 Shear Stress in a Pipe

Now build the theory. The first step needs no assumption about the regime at all — it is the force balance of Chapter 4 applied to a cylinder of fluid, and it holds for laminar and turbulent flow alike.

Writing that balance for a cylinder of radius r and length L:

(5.4) (p1 − p2)πr2 = τ · 2πrL τ = Δp r2L

So the shear stress is zero on the axis and rises in direct proportion to the radius. Setting r = R = d/2 gives the stress at the wall, which is the quantity that actually drags on the pipe:

(5.5) τw = Δp R2L = Δp d4L and reading it the other way round, Δp = 4Lτwd

Dividing (5.4) by (5.5) removes everything but the geometry:

ττw = rR (5.6)
Two things equation (5.5) is telling you
  • The pressure drop and the wall shear are the same fact twice. All the head lost to friction is paid out as shear on the pipe wall; the pipe feels it as a drag force τw × πdL pulling it downstream, which is one more thing an anchor block has to take.
  • The 4L/d is where the diameter earns its keep. For the same wall stress, a pipe of twice the bore loses half the pressure over the same length — and that is before the velocity has even been allowed to fall.

5.7 The Velocity Profile in Laminar Flow

Equation (5.4) relates shear to radius. To get any further we need a second relation between shear and velocity — and for laminar flow we have one exactly: Newton’s law of viscosity from §1.5. This is the step that fails for turbulent flow, and the reason turbulent flow has no exact solution.

Take the velocity to fall as the radius grows, so du/dr is negative while the shear stress is positive:

τ = −μ dudr (5.7)

Setting (5.4) and (5.7) equal and separating the variables:

(5.8) −μdudr = Δp r2L  ⇒  du = −Δp2μLr dr integrating, u = −Δp r24μL + C and the no-slip condition of §1.4 gives the constant: at r = R, u = 0, so C = Δp R24μL

which leaves the velocity at any radius:

ur = Δp4μL R2 − r2 (5.9)

The velocity is greatest on the axis, where r = 0:

umax = Δp R24μL = Δp d216μL (5.10)

5.8 The Hagen–Poiseuille Equation

The profile gives the velocity at every radius. The discharge is the sum of that velocity times the area it flows through, taken ring by ring as Figure 5.6 shows:

(5.11) Q = R0 ur · 2πr dr = 2πΔp4μL R0 R2r − r3 dr = πΔp2μL R2r22 − r440R = πΔp R48μL

Writing it in terms of the diameter, since that is what a pipe is sold by, gives the Hagen–Poiseuille equation:

Q = πΔp d4128μL (5.12)
The fourth power

For a given pressure drop, the discharge of a laminar pipe goes as the fourth power of the diameter. Widen the bore by 20 % and you pass twice the flow; halve it and you pass one sixteenth. This is why a partly blocked drip emitter loses its output so abruptly, why a needle gauge is so sensitive, and why blood pressure responds so strongly to small changes in vessel diameter — Poiseuille was a physician, and it was blood he was measuring.

Dividing by the area gives the mean velocity, and comparing it with equation (5.10) settles the promise made back in §3.3:

(5.13) ū = QπR2 = Δp R28μL = Δp d232μL and therefore ūumax = 12 exactly, for laminar flow in a circular pipe

Finally, rearrange (5.13) for the pressure drop and divide by ρg to put it in the head units of Chapter 3:

(5.14) Δp = 32μLūd2   ⇒   hf = 32μLūρgd2
Theory meets the measurement

Look at what equation (5.14) says about the exponent: hf is proportional to ū to the first power. That is exactly the slope of 1 that Figure 5.4 measured on the laminar branch, derived here from nothing but a force balance and Newton’s law of viscosity. The theory and the experiment agree without a single fitted constant — which is why the laminar case is worth the trouble even though you will rarely design for it.

5.9 The Friction Factor and the Darcy Equation

Turbulent flow cannot be solved the way §5.7 solved laminar flow, because Newton’s law of viscosity no longer describes the whole shear: most of the momentum is being carried by eddies, and there is no equation for those. So the turbulent case is handled the way engineers always handle what they cannot derive — by isolating the ignorance in a single coefficient and measuring it.

Start from the one relation that survived, equation (5.5), which needed no assumption about the regime:

Δp = 4Lτwd

Experiment shows that in turbulent flow the wall shear is very nearly proportional to ρu2 — which is no surprise, since that is a momentum flux and eddies transport momentum. Define a dimensionless friction factor λ to carry the constant of proportionality:

τw = λρu28 (5.15)

Substituting and dividing by ρg gives the Darcy–Weisbach equation, the single most-used formula in water engineering:

hf = λLu22gd (5.16)
What has and has not been achieved

Equation (5.16) is not a solution — it is a definition that puts every unknown into λ. Its value is that it shows what hf depends on even before λ is known: proportional to length, proportional to velocity squared, inversely proportional to diameter. Finding λ is then a separate problem, solved in §5.10 for turbulent flow — and solved exactly, right now, for laminar.

The laminar friction factor

For laminar flow we have two expressions for the same head loss: equation (5.14) from theory and equation (5.16) by definition. Setting them equal:

(5.17) 32μLuρgd2 = λLu22gd  ⇒  λ = 64μρud = 64Re

So in laminar flow the friction factor depends on the Reynolds number and on nothing else — in particular not on the roughness of the pipe. That is the entry in Table 5.2 that surprises people, and the reason is physical: in laminar flow the roughness peaks are buried inside a slow-moving layer and the fluid never sees them.

Two friction factors, and the mess they cause

Older British texts — including much of the CIVE 1400 source — use a different friction factor, written f, defined so that

hf = 4fLu22gd with f = 16Re in laminar flow

The two are related by λ = 4f. They describe the same physics and give the same answer, but a factor of four is a factor of four. This course uses λ, the Darcy friction factor, because it is what every Moody chart, every software package and every international standard prints. Whenever you take a friction factor from a book, check which one it is — if the laminar value quoted is 64/Re it is λ; if it is 16/Re it is f.

The hydraulic gradient

Dividing the head loss by the length gives the slope of the hydraulic grade line, which is how pipeline losses are usually quoted in the field:

i = hfL = λu22gd (5.18)

A designer quoting “3 metres per kilometre” is quoting i = 0.003. Because i does not depend on length, it can be worked out once for a given pipe and flow and then multiplied by whatever length the route turns out to be.

5.10 Beyond the Core — Sizing a Real Pipeline

Extension material — not examinable

This section goes beyond CIVE 1400 §4.1–4.3. §5.9 defined λ and stopped; this is how it is actually found, and what else has to be added before a number becomes a pipe order. Nothing here is examined, but all of it is second-year Irrigation Engineering, and some of it you will use before then. Any example or problem drawing on it is marked §5.10.

Roughness, and the Moody diagram

In turbulent flow the friction factor depends on two things: the Reynolds number, and the relative roughness ks/d, where ks is the average height of the bumps on the pipe wall. Plotting λ against Re for a family of relative roughnesses gives the Moody diagram, which has been the working tool of pipeline design since 1944.

The curves are drawn from the Colebrook–White equation, which fits almost the whole turbulent field:

1λ = −2 log10 ks3.7d + 2.51Re λ (5.19)

Note that λ appears on both sides: the equation cannot be rearranged and must be solved by iteration, or read off the chart, or evaluated with one of the explicit approximations built into design software. That awkwardness is the price of covering the whole field with one formula.

Corrosion, scale and biological growth raise ks over the life of a main, and the bumps also narrow the bore. An old cast-iron pipe can carry less than half its original discharge at the same head. A design that gives no allowance for ageing is a design that fails in service while every number in it is correct, which is why design codes require the friction factor for the end of the design life, not for the day of commissioning.

Losses that are not friction

Friction along the straight lengths is only part of it. Every bend, valve, contraction, tee and entry costs head as well, because each one makes the flow separate and re-mix — the mechanism of §4.10. These are called local or minor losses, and each is charged as a multiple of the velocity head:

hL = K u22g (5.20)

with K ≈ 0.5 for a sharp-edged entry, 1.0 for an exit into a tank (the Borda–Carnot result of §4.10), 0.3–0.9 for a bend, 0.2 for a fully open gate valve and 10 or more for a part-closed one. “Minor” is a bad name: on a short pumped line with many fittings they can exceed the pipe friction, and on a long main they are usually negligible. The only way to know which case you are in is to add them up.

Hazen–Williams, and why you will meet it

Irrigation practice very often uses an older empirical formula instead of Darcy–Weisbach, because it needs no iteration and no viscosity:

hf = 10.67 L Q1.852C1.852 d4.87 (5.21)

in SI units, with C the Hazen–Williams coefficient: about 150 for PVC, 140 for new steel, 130 for new cast iron and 100 or less for old. Note the exponent 1.852 on the discharge — almost, but not exactly, the square of §5.5.

Which to use

Hazen–Williams is only valid for water, at ordinary temperatures, in turbulent flow, at velocities up to about 3 m/s — the conditions of a water supply or irrigation main, which is what Hazen and Williams fitted it to. Outside those conditions it is simply wrong, and Darcy–Weisbach, which rests on the physics of §5.6 rather than on a curve fit, is always valid. Use Hazen–Williams for speed on routine water mains; use Darcy–Weisbach when the fluid is not water, the flow may be laminar, or the answer matters.

What the answer is for

The head loss is never the end of the job. It goes on to fix the pumping power, and therefore the electricity bill for the next twenty years:

P = ρgQHη with H = Hstatic + hf + ΣhL (5.22)

where η is the pump efficiency, typically 0.6–0.8. This is what makes pipe sizing an economic problem rather than a hydraulic one: a larger pipe costs more to buy once and less to pump through every year. The economic diameter is the one that minimises the sum, and it is almost always larger than the smallest pipe that would physically do the job.

Rules of thumb worth carrying
  • Design velocity in an irrigation main: 0.6 to 2.0 m/s. Below that, sediment settles out; above it, friction and surge both climb steeply and erosion begins.
  • Hydraulic gradient: 1 to 5 m per km is normal for a long main; much more than that is usually a sign the pipe is too small.
  • Check the Reynolds number once at the start, to confirm the flow is turbulent so the turbulent formulae apply. It always will be — but the one time it is not, nothing else you calculate will be right.

5.11 Worked Examples

Throughout, water at 20 °C has ρ = 1000 kg m−3 and ν = 1.0 × 10−6 m2 s−1, and g = 9.81 m s−2. Every one of these starts by finding Re, because until you know the regime you do not know which formula you are allowed to use.

Example 5.1 — The same pipe, two fluids

A 25 mm pipe carries 0.8 l s−1. Find the Reynolds number and the regime (a) for water at 20 °C and (b) for olive oil, ρ = 920 kg m−3, μ = 0.084 Pa s.

A = π(0.025)2/4 = 4.909 × 10−4 m2, so the mean velocity is the same in both cases:
u = 0.0008 / 4.909 × 10−4 = 1.63 m s−1

(a) Water. Re = ud/ν = 1.63 × 0.025 / 1.0 × 10−6 = 4.07 × 104 — well above 4000, so turbulent.

(b) Oil. First its kinematic viscosity: ν = μ/ρ = 0.084/920 = 9.13 × 10−5 m2 s−1, which is 91 times that of water.
Re = 1.63 × 0.025 / 9.13 × 10−5 = 446 — below 2000, so laminar.

Same pipe, same discharge, same velocity — and the two fluids are in completely different regimes, obeying different laws, with head losses that scale differently with velocity. Nothing but the viscosity has changed. This is the experiment Reynolds ran, and it is why the Reynolds number rather than the velocity is the thing to look at.

Example 5.2 — Laminar flow: the whole set of answers

Oil of ρ = 900 kg m−3 and μ = 0.12 Pa s flows at 0.2 l s−1 through a 20 mm pipe 50 m long. Find the regime, the pressure drop, the head loss, the maximum velocity and the shear stress at the wall.

Regime first. A = 3.142 × 10−4 m2, ū = 0.0002/3.142 × 10−4 = 0.637 m s−1.
ν = 0.12/900 = 1.333 × 10−4 m2 s−1, so Re = 0.637 × 0.020 / 1.333 × 10−4 = 95.5 — firmly laminar, so equations (5.9) to (5.14) apply.

Pressure drop, from equation (5.14):
Δp = 32μLū/d2 = 32 × 0.12 × 50 × 0.637 / (0.020)2 = 306 kPa

Head loss, in metres of the oil:
hf = Δp/ρg = 305 600 / (900 × 9.81) = 34.6 m

Maximum velocity, from equation (5.13): umax = 2ū = 1.27 m s−1, on the axis.

Wall shear stress, from equation (5.5):
τw = Δp d/4L = 305 600 × 0.020 / (4 × 50) = 30.6 Pa
Check by a different route. λ = 64/Re = 64/95.5 = 0.670, and equation (5.16) gives hf = 0.670 × 50 × 0.6372 / (2 × 9.81 × 0.020) = 34.6 m ✓. The two formulae are the same statement, so they must agree; if they do not, one of them has been copied wrongly.

Example 5.3 — A microtube drip emitter

A microtube emitter is 1.0 m of 0.6 mm-bore tubing, working at 100 kPa. For water at 25 °C take ρ = 997 kg m−3 and μ = 8.9 × 10−4 Pa s. Find the discharge, and confirm the assumption you had to make to find it.

Assume laminar and use Hagen–Poiseuille, equation (5.12):
Q = πΔp d4 / 128μL = π × 100 000 × (0.0006)4 / (128 × 8.9 × 10−4 × 1.0)

= 4.072 × 10−8 / 0.11392 = 3.57 × 10−7 m3 s−1 = 1.29 l h−1

Now check the assumption, which is not optional:
A = 2.827 × 10−7 m2, so u = 3.57 × 10−7/2.827 × 10−7 = 1.26 m s−1
Re = ρud/μ = 997 × 1.26 × 0.0006 / 8.9 × 10−4 = 850 — laminar, so the answer stands.

And a warning that falls straight out of it. In laminar flow Q ∝ 1/μ, and the viscosity of water falls by about 2 % per degree. Between a cool morning at 20 °C and a hot afternoon at 35 °C this emitter would deliver roughly 30 % more water, at the same pressure. That is exactly why commercial emitters use a turbulent labyrinth passage instead: turbulent discharge barely depends on viscosity, so it barely depends on temperature. §5.10

Example 5.4 — A turbulent main, and what the answer tells you

A 150 mm PVC main 1200 m long carries 25 l s−1 of water at 20 °C. Take λ = 0.019. Find the Reynolds number, the head loss, the hydraulic gradient and the wall shear stress — then say whether the pipe is the right size.

A = 0.01767 m2, u = 0.025/0.01767 = 1.41 m s−1.
Re = 1.41 × 0.15 / 1.0 × 10−6 = 2.12 × 105 — turbulent, so the Darcy equation applies.

Head loss, from equation (5.16):
u2/2g = 1.412/19.62 = 0.102 m, and λL/d = 0.019 × 1200/0.15 = 152
hf = 152 × 0.102 = 15.5 m

Hydraulic gradient. i = 15.5/1200 = 0.0129, that is 12.9 m per km.

Wall shear, from equation (5.15): τw = λρu2/8 = 0.019 × 1000 × 2.00/8 = 4.75 Pa

Is it the right size? No. A gradient of 12.9 m/km is far above the 1–5 m/km that a long main should run at, and 15.5 m of head thrown away is a permanent charge on the pump. Try 200 mm instead: u falls to 0.80 m s−1, λ to about 0.0175, and
hf = 0.0175 × (1200/0.2) × 0.6332/19.62 = 3.4 m, a gradient of 2.8 m/km.
One size up has cut the loss to less than a quarter — close to the (200/150)5 = 4.2 that §5.5 predicted.

Example 5.5 — What the pump has to do

A pump lifts water 18 m from a sump to a tank through 400 m of 100 mm pipe with λ = 0.022, at 12 l s−1. The line has a sharp-edged entry (K = 0.5), two bends (K = 0.4 each) and discharges into the tank (K = 1.0). Find the total head required and the power drawn at 70 % pump efficiency. §5.10

A = 7.854 × 10−3 m2, u = 0.012/7.854 × 10−3 = 1.53 m s−1, and the velocity head is u2/2g = 0.119 m.
Re = 1.53 × 0.10 / 10−6 = 1.5 × 105, turbulent ✓

Pipe friction. λL/d = 0.022 × 400/0.1 = 88, so hf = 88 × 0.119 = 10.5 m

Local losses. ΣK = 0.5 + 0.4 + 0.4 + 1.0 = 2.3, so ΣhL = 2.3 × 0.119 = 0.27 m — under 3 % of the pipe friction, which is what “minor” is supposed to mean on a long main.

Total head. H = Hstatic + hf + ΣhL = 18 + 10.5 + 0.3 = 28.8 m

Power, from equation (5.22):
P = ρgQH/η = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.012 × 28.8 / 0.70 = 4.8 kW
Of that, 3.0 kW does the useful work of lifting the water and 1.8 kW is spent purely on friction — more than a third of the bill, every hour the pump runs.

Example 5.6 — What ageing costs §5.10

A 300 mm cast-iron main 2 km long carries 80 l s−1 of water at 20 °C. When new, ks = 0.25 mm; after twenty years of tuberculation it is 1.2 mm. Find the head loss in each case, and the extra pumping power the ageing costs at 75 % efficiency.

A = 0.07069 m2, u = 0.080/0.07069 = 1.13 m s−1, u2/2g = 0.0653 m.
Re = 1.13 × 0.30/10−6 = 3.4 × 105

New pipe. ks/d = 0.25/300 = 8.3 × 10−4. From Colebrook–White, or the Moody chart at that Re, λ = 0.0198.
hf = 0.0198 × (2000/0.3) × 0.0653 = 8.6 m

Aged pipe. ks/d = 1.2/300 = 4.0 × 10−3, giving λ = 0.0287.
hf = 0.0287 × (2000/0.3) × 0.0653 = 12.5 m

The loss has risen by 3.9 m, or 45 %, without a single thing changing except the inside of the pipe. The extra power is
P = ρgQΔh/η = 9810 × 0.080 × 3.9 / 0.75 = 4.1 kW, drawn continuously for the rest of the pipe’s life.
This is the calculation that justifies designing on the aged roughness rather than the new one — and the one that pays for pipe cleaning and relining programmes.

P — power W

5.13 Self-Check

Forty-seven questions on this chapter, marked in your own browser as you answer them. Nothing is stored and nothing is sent anywhere. Each question carries the section it came from, and the summary at the end lists the sections to revise.

Work through these before the problems in §5.14. Five of the questions come from the extension material in §5.10 and are marked as such.

5.14 Problems

Take g = 9.81 m/s2, and for water at 20 °C ρ = 1000 kg/m3 and ν = 1.0 × 10−6 m2/s. Find the Reynolds number first in every problem, and say which regime you are in before choosing a formula; marks are lost for using a laminar equation on a turbulent flow whatever the arithmetic afterwards. Problems marked §5.10 draw on the extension material and are not examinable.

  1. Water at 20 °C flows at 1.2 m/s in a 50 mm pipe. Find the Reynolds number and state the regime. What velocity would be needed to make the same pipe run laminar, and is that a realistic design condition? Re = 6.0 × 104, turbulent; laminar would need u below 0.04 m/s, a discharge of 0.08 l/s — not a usable design
  2. Glycerine, ρ = 1260 kg/m3 and μ = 0.9 Pa s, flows at 2 l/s through a 100 mm pipe 30 m long. Find the Reynolds number, the regime, the head loss, the maximum velocity and the wall shear stress. Re = 36, laminar; Δp = 22.0 kPa so hf = 1.78 m of glycerine; umax = 0.509 m/s; τw = 18.3 Pa
  3. For the pipe in Problem 2, find the friction factor from λ = 64/Re and use the Darcy equation to recompute the head loss. Explain why the two answers must agree. λ = 1.80, hf = 1.78 m. They agree because λ = 64/Re was derived by equating the two expressions in the first place — it is a definition, not an independent result
  4. Oil of ρ = 850 kg/m3 and μ = 0.08 Pa s is driven through a 25 mm tube 10 m long by a pressure difference of 40 kPa. Find the discharge and the mean velocity, then confirm the assumption you made. Q = 0.479 l/s, ū = 0.977 m/s; Re = 259, laminar, so Hagen–Poiseuille was justified
  5. A 200 mm main 800 m long carries 60 l/s of water with λ = 0.020. Find the Reynolds number, the head loss, the hydraulic gradient, the wall shear stress and the power dissipated in friction. Re = 3.8 × 105; hf = 14.9 m; i = 18.6 m/km; τw = 9.1 Pa; 8.75 kW lost
  6. Repeat Problem 5 for a 250 mm pipe carrying the same discharge, with λ = 0.0185. Compare the two head losses and check the ratio against the 1/d5 rule of §5.5. hf = 4.51 m, i = 5.6 m/km, 2.65 kW lost — a saving of 6.1 kW. The ratio is 3.30, against (250/200)5 = 3.05 corrected by the ratio of the two friction factors
  7. A pipeline is to carry 40 l/s over 1500 m with a friction head loss of not more than 6 m. Taking λ = 0.020, find the smallest diameter that will do, and the nearest commercial size you would order. Rearranging the Darcy equation, d5 = 8λLQ2/π2ghf gives d = 231 mm, so order 250 mm — which gives 4.1 m, comfortably inside the limit
  8. Laminar flow in a 40 mm pipe has a maximum velocity of 0.9 m/s. Find (a) the mean velocity, (b) the discharge, (c) the velocity 10 mm from the axis, and (d) the radius at which the local velocity equals the mean. (a) 0.45 m/s (b) 0.566 l/s (c) 0.675 m/s (d) r = 0.707R = 14.1 mm — compare the 0.76R quoted for turbulent flow in §3.7
  9. A horizontal 30 mm pipe carries oil, ρ = 890 kg/m3 and μ = 0.075 Pa s, at 0.35 l/s. Gauges 15 m apart read 180 kPa and 160 kPa. Find the head loss from the gauge readings, then predict it from theory and comment on the agreement. Measured: Δp = 20 kPa so hf = 2.29 m of oil. Re = 176, laminar, and equation (5.14) predicts Δp = 19.8 kPa, hf = 2.27 m — within 1 %, which is the sort of agreement only the laminar case ever gives
  10. §5.10 A 400 mm steel main, ks = 0.045 mm, carries 150 l/s of water over 3 km. Find the Reynolds number, the relative roughness and the friction factor, then the head loss and the hydraulic gradient. Is the pipe well sized? Re = 4.8 × 105, ks/d = 1.1 × 10−4, λ = 0.0146; hf = 7.95 m, i = 2.65 m/km — comfortably inside the 1 to 5 m/km rule, so yes
  11. §5.10 Rework the head loss of Problem 10 with the Hazen–Williams equation, taking C = 140. Compare the two answers and say which you would trust and why. 8.75 m against 7.95 m, about 10 % higher. Trust Darcy–Weisbach: it rests on the force balance of §5.6 and is valid for any fluid and any regime, while Hazen–Williams is a curve fit valid only for water in turbulent flow below about 3 m/s
  12. §5.10 A pump delivering 40 l/s over 1500 m runs 2500 hours a year at 72 % efficiency, and electricity costs 4 baht per kWh. Using λ = 0.020 for a 200 mm pipe and 0.0185 for a 250 mm one, find the annual pumping cost saved by choosing the larger pipe. hf falls from 12.4 m to 3.8 m, so the pump draws 4.7 kW less; 11 800 kWh a year, about 47 000 baht a year saved — which is what has to be set against the extra cost of the pipe

The end of the course — and what it was for

Five chapters, and they are one argument. Chapter 1 said what a fluid is and measured the two properties that matter. Chapter 2 held it still and found the forces. Chapter 3 set it moving and conserved mass and energy. Chapter 4 conserved momentum and got the forces that moving fluid exerts. Chapter 5 put the viscosity of Chapter 1 back into the flow of Chapter 3, and turned the result into a number you can order a pipe with.

In year two, Irrigation Engineering takes all five for granted from the first lecture. Open channels are Chapter 3 and 4 with a free surface; pump selection is §5.10 with a catalogue; scheme design is all of it at once, with money attached.
