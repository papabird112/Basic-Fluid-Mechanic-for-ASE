3.1 Classifying a Flow

It is easy to picture a hopelessly complicated flow: the spray behind a tractor, waves breaking on a bund, the wind round a grain store. All of these can be analysed, with varying degrees of success and in some cases hardly at all. But a great many situations of practical importance are far simpler than that, and the first step in any analysis is to decide which kind of flow you are looking at.

Uniform or non-uniform, steady or unsteady

Two independent questions are being asked here. The first is about space, the second about time.

  • Uniform flow. The velocity has the same magnitude and direction at every point in the fluid.
  • Non-uniform flow. At a given instant the velocity is not the same at every point. Strictly, by that definition every fluid flowing near a solid boundary is non-uniform — the fluid at the wall must take the speed of the wall, which is usually zero. In practice, if the size and shape of the cross-section of the stream is constant, we call the flow uniform anyway.
  • Steady flow. The conditions — velocity, pressure, cross-section — may differ from point to point, but they do not change with time.
  • Unsteady flow. At some point in the fluid, conditions change with time. In practice there are always slight fluctuations in velocity and pressure; if the average values are constant we still call the flow steady.
Table 3.1. The four classes of flow, with an example of each. This course is restricted almost entirely to the first row.
ClassConditionsExample
Steady uniform Change with neither position nor time. Water flowing at constant velocity through a pipe of constant diameter.
Steady non-uniform Change from point to point but not with time. Flow in a tapering pipe fed at a constant rate — the velocity changes along its length.
Unsteady uniform Same at every point at a given instant, but changing with time. A pipe of constant diameter fed by a pump that is then switched off.
Unsteady non-uniform Change from point to point and with time. Waves travelling along an irrigation canal.
Scope of this course

Steady uniform flow is by far the simplest of the four, and it is the only class we will treat. We shall meet no genuinely non-uniform or unsteady effects, apart from one or two quasi-time-dependent problems — the emptying tank of §3.9 is the main one — which can be handled as a succession of steady states.

Compressible or incompressible

All fluids are compressible, as Chapter 1 §1.11 showed; even water changes density as pressure changes. But under steady conditions, and provided the pressure changes are small, the analysis simplifies enormously if we assume constant density.

Liquids are difficult to compress, so under most steady conditions they are treated as incompressible. Gases are easily compressed and in most cases must be treated as compressible — though not, as §1.7 noted, when they move slowly. Everything in this chapter assumes incompressible flow, and that assumption is what allows density to cancel out of the continuity equation in §3.4.

One, two or three dimensions

All real flows are three-dimensional: pressure, velocity and everything else vary in all directions. But in many cases the biggest changes happen in only one or two directions, and ignoring the others makes the analysis enormously simpler.

A flow is one-dimensional if the flow parameters at a given instant vary only in the direction of flow, and not across the cross-section. Flow in a pipe is the standard example. But notice the catch: the velocity must be zero at the pipe wall and non-zero in the centre, so there is a variation across the section. Should we treat pipe flow as two-dimensional then?

The bargain we strike

Only if very high accuracy is needed. For ordinary design we treat the flow as one-dimensional, using a mean velocity over the cross-section, and apply a correction factor where the difference matters. This is the single most useful simplification in the subject: it turns a problem in three variables into a problem in one, and it costs a few per cent.

A flow is two-dimensional if the parameters vary along the flow and in one direction at right angles to it, with the same pattern repeated on every parallel plane. Flow over a long weir is the example: along most of the weir’s length the picture is identical, and only near the two ends does it change — where, again, a correction factor is applied.

In this course we consider only steady, incompressible, one- and two-dimensional flow.

3.2 Streamlines and Streamtubes

It helps enormously to be able to picture a flow pattern, and the tool for that is the streamline: a line drawn so that at every point on it the direction of the line is the direction of the fluid velocity at that point.

Four things to know about streamlines
  • Fluid moves along a streamline, so fluid cannot cross a streamline.
  • Streamlines cannot cross each other. If they did, there would be two different velocities at the same point, which is not physically possible.
  • It follows that a particle starting on one streamline stays on that streamline.
  • Close to a solid boundary, streamlines are parallel to that boundary, because fluid neither enters nor leaves the surface.

In steady flow the position of the streamlines does not change with time. In unsteady flow it does — another reason we restrict ourselves to steady flow.

The streamtube

A very useful technique is to consider only a part of the flow, isolated from the rest. Imagine a tubular surface made up of streamlines, running along the direction of flow. That surface is a streamtube.

Because fluid cannot cross a streamline, it cannot cross the wall of a streamtube either. A streamtube can therefore be treated like a solid-walled pipe — fluid enters at one end and leaves at the other, and nothing escapes in between. That is exactly the property we need in §3.4.

Two cautions. A streamtube is not a pipe: in unsteady flow its walls move with time, and even in steady flow its “wall” is moving with the fluid rather than standing still. But for a steady flow, for the purposes of accounting for what goes in and what comes out, the analogy is exact.

3.3 Flow Rate: Mass Flow and Discharge

Mass flow rate

How would you measure the rate at which water flows from a tap? The obvious way is to put a bucket under it, time how long it takes to collect a known mass of water, and divide:

mass flow rate = ṁ = mass of fluid collectedtime taken (3.1)

If 8.0 kg is collected in 12 s, then ṁ = 8.0/12 = 0.67 kg s−1. The dot over the m is a standard notation meaning “per unit time”.

Volume flow rate — discharge

More commonly we want the volume flow rate, universally called the discharge and given the symbol Q:

Q = volume of fluidtime taken = ṁρ (3.2)

Its SI unit is m3 s−1. Two other units are in everyday use and you must be fluent in both: the litre per second (1 m3 s−1 = 1000 l s−1), and in irrigation practice the cubic metre per hour (1 m3 s−1 = 3600 m3 h−1).

Discharge and mean velocity

Now connect the discharge to the velocity. Consider a pipe of cross-sectional area A through which fluid moves at a uniform velocity u. In a time t a particle at the inlet travels a distance ut, so the volume that has passed the inlet is Aut, and the volume per unit time is:

Q = A ū (3.3)
What “mean velocity” really means

This is the one-dimensional bargain of §3.1, written as an equation. Because the velocity across a real section is not uniform, we define ū = Q/A and work with that. For fully developed laminar pipe flow ū = 0.5 umax; for turbulent pipe flow, which is what you meet in practice, the profile is much flatter and ū ≈ 0.80–0.85 umax. Chapter 5 explains why the two differ so much. From here on the bar is dropped and u means the mean velocity unless stated otherwise.

Example 3.1 — From bucket to velocity

An empty bucket weighs 2.0 kg. After 7 s of collecting water from a 25 mm-diameter pipe the bucket weighs 8.0 kg. Find the mass flow rate, the discharge and the mean velocity in the pipe. Take ρ = 1000 kg m−3.

Mass of water collected = 8.0 − 2.0 = 6.0 kg, so ṁ = 6.0/7 = 0.857 kg s−1.

Q = ṁ/ρ = 0.857/1000 = 8.57 × 10−4 m3 s−1 = 0.857 l s−1.

A = πd2/4 = π(0.025)2/4 = 4.909 × 10−4 m2.

u = Q/A = 8.57 × 10−4 / 4.909 × 10−4 = 1.75 m s−1

3.4 The Continuity Equation

Matter cannot be created or destroyed. Applied to a fixed region of a flow — a control volume — that statement becomes an accounting identity:

mass entering per unit time = mass leaving per unit time + increase of mass in the control volume per unit time

For steady flow nothing accumulates: the mass in the control volume is constant, so the last term is zero and

mass entering per unit time = mass leaving per unit time (3.4)

Apply this to the streamtube of Figure 3.1(b). Nothing crosses the walls, so all the mass enters through face 1 and leaves through face 2:

(3.5) ρ1A1u1 = ρ2A2u2 = constant = ṁ

This holds for any streamtube in steady flow, whatever the fluid. Now bring in the second assumption of §3.1: the flow is incompressible, so ρ1 = ρ2 and density cancels:

(3.6) A1u1 = A2u2 = Q = constant
The most useful equation in the subject

Equation (3.6) says: in steady incompressible flow the discharge is the same at every section. Squeeze the area and the velocity must rise in exact proportion. It needs no information about pressure, friction, or what the fluid is — only that mass is conserved and the density is constant. It is the first equation to write down in almost every problem in this course.

A tapering pipe

If a pipe of diameter d1 reduces to d2, then because A = πd2/4 the areas are in the ratio of the squares of the diameters:

(3.7) u2 = u1 A1A2 = u1 d1d22

So halving the diameter quadruples the velocity. This squared dependence is worth remembering; it is behind most of the surprises in pipework design, and behind the large pressure drops you will compute in Chapter 5.

Junctions: more than two faces

If a junction has one inlet and two outlets, the total mass flow in must equal the total mass flow out:

(3.8) ρ1Q1 = ρ2Q2 + ρ3Q3 and if the fluid is the same throughout, A1u1 = A2u2 + A3u3

Example 3.2 — A tapering section and a branch

Water flows at 30 l s−1 in a 150 mm pipe which contracts to 100 mm and then divides into two 75 mm branches. If one branch carries twice the discharge of the other, find the mean velocity in the 150 mm, the 100 mm and each 75 mm pipe.

Q = 30 l s−1 = 0.030 m3 s−1.

A1 = π(0.150)2/4 = 0.01767 m2, so u1 = 0.030/0.01767 = 1.70 m s−1.

A2 = π(0.100)2/4 = 7.854 × 10−3 m2, so u2 = 0.030/7.854 × 10−3 = 3.82 m s−1. Check with (3.7): 1.70 × (150/100)2 = 3.82 ✓

At the junction Qa + Qb = 0.030 with Qb = 2Qa, so 3Qa = 0.030, giving Qa = 0.010 and Qb = 0.020 m3 s−1.

A75 = π(0.075)2/4 = 4.418 × 10−3 m2.

ua = 0.010/4.418 × 10−3 = 2.26 m s−1; ub = 0.020/4.418 × 10−3 = 4.53 m s−1

3.5 Work, Energy and the Bernoulli Equation

Continuity told us how fast the fluid moves. It says nothing about pressure. For that we need a second conservation law — of energy — and the result is the most famous equation in fluid mechanics.

The three forms of energy

Take an element of fluid of mass m moving along a streamline. It carries energy in three forms:

FormEnergy of the elementWhere it comes from
Kinetic energy ½mu2 Its motion.
Potential energy mgz Its height z above an arbitrary datum.
Pressure energy pm/ρ Work done on the element by the fluid behind it as it is pushed forward — often called flow work.

The third one is the unfamiliar one, so derive it. To push the element a distance δs through a section of area A, the fluid behind it exerts a force pA and therefore does work pAδs. But Aδs is the volume of the element, which is m/ρ. So the work done on it is pm/ρ.

The assumptions

The Bernoulli equation is valid only if
  1. the flow is steady;
  2. the density is constant — the fluid is incompressible;
  3. friction losses are negligible — the fluid is ideal, as defined in §1.4;
  4. the equation relates two points on the same streamline;
  5. no energy is added or removed between the two points — no pump, no turbine.

Assumption 3 is the one that fails first in real pipework. Chapter 5 puts the friction back in. Assumption 4 is relaxed in practice for the flow in a single streamtube, where every streamline carries the same total energy.

The derivation

With no losses and no work added, the total energy of the element is the same at any two points on the streamline. Writing points 1 and 2:

(3.10) p1mρ + ½mu12 + mgz1 = p2mρ + ½mu22 + mgz2 divide throughout by m:   p1ρ + u122 + gz1 = p2ρ + u222 + gz2 divide throughout by g:   p1ρg + u122g + z1 = p2ρg + u222g + z2 = H = constant

Equation (3.10) is the Bernoulli equation in energy per unit mass (J kg−1). Multiply it by ρ instead and you get the pressure form, energy per unit volume:

(3.12) p1 + ½ρu12 + ρgz1 = p2 + ½ρu22 + ρgz2

But form (3.11) is the one engineers use, because every term has the dimension of length — it is measured in metres of the fluid, exactly as in §2.4. That is what makes it drawable, and the next section draws it.

Check the dimensions yourself

[p/ρg] = (ML−1T−2) ÷ (ML−3 · LT−2) = L    [u2/2g] = (L2T−2) ÷ (LT−2) = L    [z] = L

All three terms are lengths, so the equation is dimensionally homogeneous — the test of §1.3. If your version of the Bernoulli equation ever fails this check, you have dropped a g or a ρ.

3.6 The Four Heads, and the Grade Lines

Each term of equation (3.11) has a name, and each can be measured with a tube. Learn the names: the rest of the chapter is written in them.

z — potential (or elevation) head m
Height above the chosen datum. It costs nothing to move the datum; only differences matter.
p/ρg — pressure head m
The height the fluid would rise in a piezometer, as in §2.5.
u2/2g — velocity (or dynamic) head m
The extra height the fluid rises if it is also brought to rest.
H — total head m
The sum of the three. Constant along a streamline, for an ideal fluid.

Two further combinations have their own names, because they are the two lines you draw on every pipe profile:

  • The piezometric head, p/ρg + z, plotted along the pipe, gives the hydraulic grade line (HGL). It is the level water would stand at in a row of piezometers tapped into the pipe wall.
  • The total head, H, plotted along the pipe, gives the energy grade line (EGL, also called the total energy line). It sits above the HGL by the velocity head u2/2g at every section.
How to read the two lines
  • The EGL is always above the HGL, by the velocity head.
  • They coincide only where the velocity is zero — at the still surface of a reservoir.
  • Where the HGL falls below the pipe, the pressure there is below atmospheric. That is the warning sign for cavitation (§1.11) and for air being drawn in through joints.
  • A pump makes both lines step up; a turbine makes them step down.

Where does the pressure go?

Notice what equation (3.11) implies for a horizontal pipe, where z1 = z2 and the elevation term drops out:

p1ρg + u122g = p2ρg + u222g (3.13)

Fast flow means low pressure. Combine this with continuity — narrow means fast — and you have the working principle of every device in §3.7 to §3.10: constrict the flow, measure the pressure drop, and deduce the discharge.

3.7 The Pitot and Pitot-Static Tubes

Consider a bent tube pushed into a flowing stream with its open mouth facing upstream. Fluid runs up the tube until the pressure at the bottom balances the column above, and then it stops. With the fluid in the tube at rest, the fluid arriving at the mouth is also brought to rest. That point is a stagnation point, and the pressure there is the stagnation pressure.

The simple Pitot tube

Apply the Bernoulli equation along the central streamline from a point 1 in the undisturbed flow to the stagnation point 2, taking both at the same level so z1 = z2, and with u2 = 0:

(3.14) p1ρg + u122g = p2ρg p2 = p1 + ½ρu12

The stagnation pressure exceeds the static pressure by exactly the dynamic pressure ½ρu2. That excess raises the column in the tube by the velocity head h = u2/2g, so if you can read h:

u = 2gh (3.15)

The Pitot-static tube

A simple Pitot tube gives the stagnation pressure only. To get the velocity you also need the static pressure at the same point, and reading the two with separate instruments is awkward and inaccurate. The Pitot-static tube solves this by combining them in one body: a central tube facing upstream senses p2, and a ring of small holes in the side, parallel to the flow, senses the static pressure p1. Connect the two to opposite limbs of a manometer and the reading is the difference.

Working through the manometer analysis of §2.5, with a gauge fluid of density ρman and a reading h:

(3.16) p2 − p1 = gh(ρman − ρ) and since p2 − p1 = ½ρu2, u = 2gh ρman − ρρ

In practice the instrument is not quite ideal — the holes disturb the flow slightly — so a coefficient Cv close to 1 (typically 0.98–1.00 for a well-made instrument) multiplies the answer. The Pitot-static tube is the standard instrument for measuring point velocity in a pipe or a channel, and it is how the airspeed of an aircraft is measured.

One point, not the whole pipe

A Pitot tube measures the velocity at the point of its mouth, not the mean velocity. To get the discharge you either traverse the section and integrate, or place the tube where the local velocity is known to equal the mean — in turbulent pipe flow, about 0.76 R from the axis. In an open channel the standard practice is to read at 0.6 of the depth below the surface, or to average readings at 0.2 and 0.8 of the depth.

3.8 The Venturi Meter

The Venturi meter is a device for measuring discharge in a pipe, and it is the most direct possible application of §3.6: it deliberately constricts the flow, and reads the resulting pressure drop.

Deriving the meter equation

Apply the Bernoulli equation between section 1 upstream and section 2 at the throat, assuming no loss:

(3.17) p1ρg + u122g + z1 = p2ρg + u222g + z2 and continuity gives u1 = A2u2A1 substituting and solving for u2: u2 = 2g (p1 − p2)⁄ρg + z1 − z21 − (A2⁄A1)2 so the ideal discharge is Qideal = A2u2

Why the manometer makes the slope irrelevant

Equation (3.17) still contains the elevations, which is inconvenient if the meter is installed on a slope. But work the manometer through, exactly as in §2.5, and the elevation terms cancel:

(3.19) p1 − p2ρg + z1 − z2 = hρmanρ − 1

Putting (3.19) into (3.17) and (3.18), and inserting a coefficient of discharge Cd to account for the small real loss between the two tappings:

(3.20) Q = Cd A1A2A12 − A22 2ghρmanρ − 1
Three things equation (3.20) is telling you
  • The slope of the meter does not matter. Whether the Venturi is horizontal, vertical or inclined, the same manometer reading means the same discharge. This is a real practical advantage over a meter whose tappings are read separately.
  • Q depends on √h, not on h. Halving the discharge cuts the reading to a quarter, so a single meter has a limited useful range — typically about 4:1.
  • Cd is high, about 0.97–0.99, because the losses between inlet and throat are genuinely small. The Venturi is one of the most accurate simple flow meters there is.

The permanent head loss

The pressure recovers in the divergent cone, but not completely. The permanent head loss of a well-designed Venturi is only about 10–20 % of the throat differential — far less than an orifice plate (§3.11), which is why a Venturi is preferred wherever pumping energy is expensive, despite costing far more to install.

3.9 Flow Through an Orifice

A tank with a small sharp-edged hole in its side is the simplest flow device of all, and the analysis is the cleanest illustration of the Bernoulli equation in the whole course.

Torricelli’s result

Take point 1 at the free surface and point 2 at the vena contracta. At both points the pressure is atmospheric, so the pressure terms cancel. If the tank is large, the surface falls so slowly that u1 ≈ 0. Then equation (3.11) gives:

(3.21) 0 + 0 + z1 = 0 + u222g + z2 u2 = 2g(z1 − z2) = 2gh

This is the theoretical velocity of efflux, first stated by Torricelli in 1643. It is exactly the speed a stone would reach if dropped from the free surface through the height h — which is precisely what conservation of energy demands.

The three coefficients

Real jets fall short of the ideal in two separate ways, and each has its own coefficient.

Table 3.2. The three orifice coefficients, with typical values for a sharp-edged circular orifice discharging to atmosphere.
CoefficientDefinitionTypical valueWhat it accounts for
Coefficient of contraction Cc Ac / A — area of vena contracta ÷ area of orifice 0.61–0.66 The sideways momentum the fluid already has when it reaches the hole.
Coefficient of velocity Cv uactual / uideal 0.97–0.99 Friction at the edge of the hole.
Coefficient of discharge Cd Cc × Cv 0.60–0.65 Both together — the only one you usually need.
Bell-mouthed (rounded) orifice: the contraction is almost eliminated, Cc ≈ 1 and Cd ≈ 0.97.

The actual discharge is therefore:

(3.22) Qactual = Ac uactual = CcA · Cv 2gh Q = Cd A 2gh
Why the vena contracta and not the hole?

Students often apply the Bernoulli equation at the plane of the hole itself. That is wrong, and understanding why is worth the effort: at the hole the streamlines are still strongly curved, so there is a pressure gradient across the jet and the pressure is not atmospheric. Only at the vena contracta, where the streamlines have become parallel, is the pressure atmospheric across the whole section. Apply the Bernoulli equation where the streamlines are parallel.

Time for a tank to empty

Here is the one quasi-unsteady problem promised in §3.1. As a tank drains, h falls, so Q falls, so the tank drains ever more slowly. Treat each instant as a steady state.

In a small time δt, the level falls by −δh and the volume leaving is Qδt. If the tank has plan area At:

(3.23) Qδt = −Atδh δt = − AtδhCdA 2gh integrating from h1 down to h2: t = 2Ath1 − h2CdA 2g

Note that emptying the tank completely (h2 = 0) takes twice as long as emptying the top half of the head — not four times, as the √h law might suggest at first glance. Equation (3.23) applies only to a tank of constant plan area; if At varies with h it must stay inside the integral.

3.10 Notches and Weirs

A notch is an opening in the side of a tank or reservoir which extends above the free surface. It is, in effect, a large orifice with no top edge. A weir is the same thing on the scale of a river or a canal — usually a wall across the channel with water flowing over it. For an irrigation engineer this is the most important section of the chapter: a weir is how discharge in an open channel is actually measured in the field.

Because the opening extends to the surface, the velocity varies from top to bottom of the opening. There is no single h, so we cannot simply write Q = CdA√(2gh). Instead we integrate.

The general notch equation

Consider a horizontal strip of the opening, of thickness δh at a depth h below the free surface, and of width b which may itself depend on h. Treating the strip as a small orifice:

(3.24) velocity through the strip = 2gh discharge through the strip = bδh 2gh integrating from the crest (h = H) up to the surface (h = 0): Qideal = 2g H0 b h1⁄2 dh

Everything now depends on the shape of the notch, through b(h).

The rectangular notch

Here the width is constant, b = B, so it comes outside the integral:

(3.25) Qideal = B 2g H0 h1⁄2 dh = 23 B 2g H3⁄2 and with a coefficient of discharge: Q = 23 Cd B 2g H3⁄2

Typical Cd for a sharp-crested rectangular weir is 0.62. The head is raised to the power 3⁄2, which is a much gentler law than the H1/2 of an orifice — that is precisely what makes a weir a good measuring device over a wide range of flows.

The V-notch

For a triangular notch of included angle θ, geometry gives the width of the strip at depth h below the surface as b = 2(H − h) tan(θ/2). Substituting into (3.24) and integrating:

(3.26) Qideal = 2 tanθ2 2g H0 (H − h)h1⁄2 dh = 2 tanθ2 2g 23Hh3⁄2 − 25h5⁄20H = 815 tanθ2 2g H5⁄2 Q = 815 Cd tanθ2 2g H5⁄2

Typical Cd is 0.58. For the standard 90° V-notch, tan 45° = 1 and equation (3.26) collapses to the field formula every irrigation technician knows:

Q ≈ 1.37 H5⁄2   (Q in m3 s−1, H in m) (3.27)
Which notch for which job?
  • The V-notch is far more sensitive at small discharges: with Q ∝ H5/2, a small flow still produces a readable head. Use it for Q below roughly 50 l s−1 — farm laterals, drainage outfalls, seepage measurements.
  • The rectangular notch passes far more water for the same head. Use it on main canals where the flow is large and the freeboard is limited.
  • The Cipoletti notch is a trapezoidal compromise: its 1:4 side slopes are chosen so that the extra area at the sides exactly compensates for the end contractions, so no end correction is needed and it obeys the simple H3/2 law. That convenience is why it is standard on irrigation canals throughout the world.

End contractions and the velocity of approach

Two corrections are applied in practice, and the exam expects you to know they exist.

End contractions. If the notch is narrower than the channel, the streamlines must turn in from the sides, contracting the effective width. The Francis correction reduces the width by 0.1H for each end contraction:

Beffective = B − 0.1nH   where n = 0, 1 or 2 end contractions (3.28)

Velocity of approach. The derivation assumed the water upstream was at rest. If the approach channel is small the water is already moving at ua, carrying a velocity head ha = ua2/2g which adds to the effective head:

Q = 23 CdB 2g [(H + ha)3⁄2 − ha3⁄2] (3.29)

In a properly designed installation, with a still pond upstream, ha is negligible and (3.25) is used directly. That is why the standards insist on an approach pool.

Installing a measuring weir — the rules that make the formula true
  • The crest must be sharp (about 1–2 mm thick, chamfered 45° downstream), so the nappe springs clear.
  • The nappe must be ventilated — air must reach the underside. A clinging nappe reads high by 5–10 %.
  • The weir must be free-flowing, not drowned: the downstream water level must stay below the crest.
  • H is measured upstream of the drawdown, at least 4H back from the crest, in a stilling well.
  • The approach pool should be at least 3 H deep and the flow in it slow and smooth.

Get any of these wrong and the coefficient in the table no longer applies — the commonest reason field measurements disagree with the design.

3.11 Beyond the Core — Measuring Flow in the Field

Extension material — not examinable

This section goes beyond CIVE 1400 §3.1–3.5. It is here because the devices in §3.7–§3.10 are the classical ones, and a graduate walking onto an irrigation scheme today will meet several others. Nothing here is examined, but the ideas are worth ten minutes. Any example or problem drawing on this section is marked §3.11.

The orifice plate

An orifice plate is a flat disc with a central hole, clamped between two pipe flanges. Pressure tappings sit one pipe diameter upstream and half a diameter downstream, and the discharge follows exactly the Venturi formula (3.20) with a different coefficient:

Q = Cd A1A2A12 − A22 2ghρmanρ − 1   with Cd ≈ 0.6 (3.30)

The comparison with the Venturi is instructive, and it is a real engineering trade-off:

Venturi meterOrifice plate
Cd0.97–0.99≈ 0.6
Permanent head loss10–20 % of the differential50–70 %
Cost and lengthHigh; needs a long straight runVery low; fits between two flanges
Wear and blockageRobustSharp edge wears; traps debris
Best used forContinuous metering where pumping energy is costlyOccasional metering, clean fluid, tight budget

Meters with no moving parts and no pressure loss

  • Electromagnetic flowmeter. A coil sets up a magnetic field across the pipe; the moving water is a conductor, so by Faraday’s law it generates a voltage proportional to the mean velocity. It has a clear full bore — no obstruction, no head loss, no blockage — but the liquid must be electrically conductive, which water in irrigation always is. Accuracy is typically ±0.5 %.
  • Ultrasonic transit-time meter. Two transducers send pulses diagonally across the flow, one upstream and one downstream. The pulse travelling with the flow arrives sooner; the difference in transit time gives the velocity. Clamp-on versions need no penetration of the pipe at all, which is why they are the standard tool for auditing an existing pipeline.
  • Doppler meter. Reads the frequency shift from particles or bubbles in the flow. Cheaper than transit-time, and unlike it, needs the water to be dirty — a good match for canal and drainage water.

Measuring an open channel without a weir

  • The Parshall flume. A weir dams the channel and traps sediment behind it; a flume does not. The Parshall flume narrows the channel to a throat and raises the floor, forcing the flow through the critical condition, after which a single upstream depth reading gives the discharge. It costs very little head — typically a quarter of what a weir costs — so it is the standard structure on flat irrigation systems with little head to spare.
  • The velocity–area method. Divide the cross-section into vertical panels, measure the mean velocity in each with a current meter or propeller (at 0.6 depth, or averaging 0.2 and 0.8), multiply by the panel area, and add. This is how river gauging stations are calibrated, and it is the method you will use in the laboratory.
  • Dilution gauging. Inject a tracer of known concentration at a steady rate and measure the diluted concentration downstream; the ratio gives the discharge. It needs no geometry at all, which makes it the only practical method on a steep, rocky, turbulent stream.

A practical warning about Cd

Every coefficient quoted in this chapter is a typical value for a well-made device in a clean, steady, fully developed flow. In the field, Cd drifts: the edge of an orifice rounds off with wear (and Cd rises), a weir crest collects algae, an upstream bend leaves a swirl that persists for twenty diameters. Standards such as ISO 5167 for pipe meters and ISO 1438 for thin-plate weirs exist precisely to pin down the installation conditions under which the published coefficients can be trusted.

The engineering habit worth forming: a flow measurement is only as good as its installation. Quoting Q to four significant figures from a meter installed two diameters downstream of an elbow is self-deception.

3.12 Worked Examples

Examples 3.1 and 3.2 appear in §3.3 and §3.4, where they belong. The six here bring continuity and the Bernoulli equation together, which is how almost every real problem arrives. Throughout, g = 9.81 m s−2 and ρ for water is 1000 kg m−3.

Example 3.3 — A tapering pipe that also rises

Water flows at 40 l s−1 through a pipe that reduces from 200 mm to 100 mm diameter. The 100 mm section is 3.0 m above the 200 mm section. The gauge pressure at the larger section is 250 kPa. Neglecting losses, find the pressure at the smaller section.

Continuity first. A1 = π(0.200)2/4 = 0.03142 m2, u1 = 0.040/0.03142 = 1.273 m s−1.
A2 = π(0.100)2/4 = 7.854 × 10−3 m2, u2 = 0.040/7.854 × 10−3 = 5.093 m s−1.

The heads at section 1, taking the datum at section 1:
pressure head = 250 000/(1000 × 9.81) = 25.484 m
velocity head = 1.2732/(2 × 9.81) = 0.083 m
potential head = 0
H = 25.567 m

At section 2 the total head is the same (no losses), but velocity head = 5.0932/19.62 = 1.322 m and potential head = 3.0 m, so
pressure head = 25.567 − 1.322 − 3.000 = 21.245 m

p2 = 21.245 × 1000 × 9.81 = 208 kPa gauge
The pressure has dropped by 42 kPa: 13 kPa spent on accelerating the water and 29 kPa on lifting it 3 m.

Example 3.4 — A Pitot-static tube

A Pitot-static tube on the axis of a water pipe is connected to a mercury U-tube manometer, which reads 25 mm. Take Cv = 0.98 and ρHg = 13 600 kg m−3. What is the local velocity?

From equation (3.16):
uideal = √[2 × 9.81 × 0.025 × (13 600 − 1000)/1000] = √(0.4905 × 12.6) = √6.180 = 2.486 m s−1

Applying the coefficient: u = 0.98 × 2.486.

u = 2.44 m s−1 on the axis.
Note this is the maximum velocity, not the mean. If the flow is turbulent, the mean is roughly 0.82 × 2.44 ≈ 2.0 m s−1, and that is the figure to use for the discharge.

Example 3.5 — A Venturi meter

A Venturi meter with inlet diameter 150 mm and throat diameter 75 mm carries water. A mercury U-tube manometer between the inlet and throat reads 180 mm. Taking Cd = 0.97, find the discharge and the throat velocity. Does the answer change if the meter is mounted on a 30° slope?

A1 = 0.017671 m2, A2 = 4.4179 × 10−3 m2.
A1A2 ÷ √(A12 − A22) = 7.807 × 10−5 / 0.017110 = 4.563 × 10−3 m2

The head term: 2gh(ρman/ρ − 1) = 2 × 9.81 × 0.180 × (13.6 − 1) = 44.50, so its root is 6.671 m s−1.

From equation (3.20): Q = 0.97 × 4.563 × 10−3 × 6.671.

Q = 0.0295 m3 s−1 = 29.5 l s−1
Throat velocity u2 = Q/A2 = 0.0295/4.4179 × 10−3 = 6.68 m s−1
No — the slope makes no difference. The elevation terms cancelled in equation (3.19) when the manometer was analysed, so the same reading means the same discharge at any inclination.

Example 3.6 — A tank emptying through an orifice

A rectangular tank 2.0 m × 1.5 m in plan has a sharp-edged orifice 50 mm in diameter in its base, with Cd = 0.62. The water is initially 2.5 m deep. Find (a) the initial discharge, (b) the time for the level to fall to 1.0 m, and (c) the time to empty the tank completely.

(a) A = π(0.050)2/4 = 1.9635 × 10−3 m2.
Q = CdA√(2gh) = 0.62 × 1.9635 × 10−3 × √(2 × 9.81 × 2.5) = 0.62 × 1.9635 × 10−3 × 7.004 = 8.53 × 10−3 m3 s−1 = 8.53 l s−1

For (b) and (c) use equation (3.23) with At = 2.0 × 1.5 = 3.0 m2.
The denominator CdA√(2g) = 0.62 × 1.9635 × 10−3 × 4.429 = 5.392 × 10−3.

(b) t = 2 × 3.0 × (√2.5 − √1.0) ÷ 5.392 × 10−3 = 6.0 × (1.5811 − 1.0000) / 5.392 × 10−3 = 647 s = 10.8 min

(c) t = 6.0 × (1.5811 − 0) ÷ 5.392 × 10−3 = 1760 s = 29.3 min
The first 1.5 m of depth takes 11 minutes; the last 1.0 m takes 18. The tank runs out of head long before it runs out of water.

Example 3.7 — Choosing between a V-notch and a rectangular notch

A farm lateral is to be gauged. Compare a 90° V-notch (Cd = 0.58) with a 0.6 m-wide rectangular notch (Cd = 0.62), both at a head of 0.25 m. Then find the head each would give at a discharge of 5 l s−1, and say which you would install.

V-notch at H = 0.25 m, from equation (3.26) with tan 45° = 1:
Q = 8⁄15 × 0.58 × 1 × 4.429 × 0.255/2 = 1.370 × 0.03125 = 0.0428 m3 s−1 = 42.8 l s−1

Rectangular notch at H = 0.25 m, from equation (3.25):
Q = 2⁄3 × 0.62 × 0.6 × 4.429 × 0.253/2 = 1.0985 × 0.125 = 0.137 m3 s−1 = 137 l s−1
At the same head the rectangular notch passes more than three times the flow.

Now run it backwards at Q = 5 l s−1.
V-notch: H5/2 = 0.005/1.370 = 3.649 × 10−3, so H = 106 mm.
Rectangular: H3/2 = 0.005/1.0985 = 4.552 × 10−3, so H = 27 mm.

Install the V-notch. A 1 mm error in reading the hook gauge is about 1 % of the V-notch head but nearly 4 % of the rectangular head — and because Q ∝ H3/2, that becomes a 5–6 % error in the discharge. The V-notch spreads a small flow over a large, readable head, which is exactly what a lateral needs. On the main canal, where the flow is ten times larger, the rectangular notch wins for the opposite reason: the V-notch would need an impossibly deep cut.

Example 3.8 — A siphon, and how high it may go

A 75 mm siphon pipe carries water from a reservoir over a bund and discharges freely to atmosphere 3.5 m below the reservoir surface. The crest of the siphon is 1.8 m above the reservoir surface. Neglecting losses, find the discharge and the pressure at the crest. What is the greatest height the crest could be raised to, if the water is at 20 °C (pv = 2.34 kPa) and atmospheric pressure is 101.3 kPa?

The discharge comes from the surface and the outlet, both at atmospheric pressure, with the surface at rest. Datum at the reservoir surface, outlet at z = −3.5 m:
u = √(2 × 9.81 × 3.5) = 8.29 m s−1

A = π(0.075)2/4 = 4.4179 × 10−3 m2, so Q = 8.29 × 4.4179 × 10−3 = 0.0366 m3 s−1 = 36.6 l s−1
Notice that the crest height does not appear: the discharge is set by the outlet level alone.

The pressure at the crest. The pipe has one diameter throughout, so by continuity the velocity there is also 8.29 m s−1 and the velocity head is 8.292/19.62 = 3.50 m. Applying the Bernoulli equation from the surface to the crest, at z = +1.8 m:
0 = pc/ρg + 3.50 + 1.80, so pc/ρg = −5.30 m

pc = −5.30 × 9810 = −52.0 kPa gauge, that is 101.3 − 52.0 = 49.3 kPa absolute — comfortably above the vapour pressure, so the siphon runs.

The limit. Cavitation begins when the absolute pressure reaches pv. In head terms, atmospheric = 101 300/9810 = 10.33 m and the vapour pressure = 2340/9810 = 0.24 m, so the pressure head at the crest may fall no lower than −(10.33 − 0.24) = −10.09 m. With 3.50 m of that already spent on the velocity head, the crest can be at most zmax = 10.09 − 3.50 = 6.6 m above the reservoir surface.
In practice a siphon is never designed above about 5 m, because friction losses (Chapter 5) eat into the margin and dissolved air comes out of solution long before the water boils. §1.11

3.15 Problems

Take g = 9.81 m/s2, ρwater = 1000 kg/m3 and ρHg = 13 600 kg/m3 unless told otherwise. Treat every fluid as ideal — friction arrives in Chapter 5. Answers are given so that you can check yourself; the marks are in the working. Problems marked §3.11 draw on the extension material and are not examinable.

  1. Water flows in a 100 mm pipe at a mean velocity of 2.5 m/s. Find the discharge in l/s and in m3/h, and the mass flow rate. 19.6 l/s = 70.7 m3/h; 19.6 kg/s
  2. A 200 mm pipe carrying 60 l/s reduces to 125 mm and then divides into two 80 mm branches which share the flow equally. Find the mean velocity in each of the four pipes. 1.91 m/s; 4.89 m/s; 5.97 m/s in each branch
  3. A horizontal pipe reduces from 150 mm to 75 mm diameter and carries 25 l/s. The gauge pressure at the larger section is 180 kPa. Find the pressure at the smaller section, and sketch the EGL and HGL along the pipe, marking which one changes and which does not. 165 kPa — a drop of 15 kPa. The EGL is horizontal; the HGL steps down by 1.53 m at the contraction
  4. A Pitot-static tube on the axis of a 200 mm water pipe is connected to a mercury manometer reading 40 mm. Take Cv = 0.98. Find the velocity on the axis, and hence estimate the discharge if the flow is turbulent with a mean velocity 0.82 times the maximum. 3.08 m/s on the axis; mean 2.53 m/s, so Q ≈ 79 l/s
  5. A Venturi meter of inlet diameter 200 mm and throat diameter 100 mm has Cd = 0.98. A mercury manometer between inlet and throat reads 250 mm. Find the discharge and the throat velocity. 62.5 l/s; 7.96 m/s at the throat
  6. The meter in Problem 5 is replaced by an orifice plate of the same throat area, with Cd = 0.60. What manometer reading would the same discharge now give? Comment on what this means for the size of manometer needed. 667 mm of mercury — nearly three times the reading, because Q ∝ Cd√h and Cd has fallen by a factor of 1.63
  7. A sharp-edged orifice 40 mm in diameter discharges from a tank under a constant head of 1.8 m. Measurement gives Cd = 0.61 and Cv = 0.97. Find the discharge, the actual velocity at the vena contracta, the coefficient of contraction and the area of the vena contracta. 4.56 l/s; 5.76 m/s; Cc = 0.629; Ac = 7.90 × 10−4 m2
  8. A cylindrical tank 1.5 m in diameter contains water 3.0 m deep above a 40 mm sharp-edged orifice in its base, with Cd = 0.61. Find the time for the level to fall to 1.0 m, and the time to empty the tank completely. 762 s = 12.7 min; 1803 s = 30.0 min
  9. A sharp-crested rectangular notch 0.9 m wide, with Cd = 0.62, is working under a head of 0.30 m. Find the discharge, first ignoring end contractions and then applying the Francis correction for two contractions. By what percentage do the two answers differ? 271 l/s ignoring them; 253 l/s with the correction — 6.7 % lower
  10. A 90° V-notch has Cd = 0.58. (a) Find the discharge at a head of 0.20 m. (b) What head corresponds to a discharge of 30 l/s? (c) If the head at 0.20 m is read 3 mm too high, what percentage error does that cause in the discharge? (a) 24.5 l/s (b) 0.217 m (c) 3.8 % too high — the 5/2 power multiplies a 1.5 % reading error by 2.5
  11. A siphon of 50 mm bore carries water from a reservoir over a bund and discharges freely 4.0 m below the reservoir surface. The crest is 2.0 m above the surface. Neglecting losses, find the discharge and the gauge pressure at the crest. Would raising the crest change the discharge? 17.4 l/s; −58.9 kPa gauge at the crest. No — the discharge depends only on the outlet level, though the crest pressure falls further and eventually the siphon breaks
  12. §3.11 An orifice plate with a 75 mm bore is fitted in a 150 mm pipe carrying water, with Cd = 0.60. A mercury manometer across it reads 300 mm. Find the discharge. Then, taking the permanent head loss as 60 % of the measured differential, find that loss in metres of water and the pumping power it wastes. 23.6 l/s; differential 3.78 m of water, permanent loss 2.27 m = 22.3 kPa, wasting about 525 W continuously
