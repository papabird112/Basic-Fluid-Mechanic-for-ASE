Forces in Static Fluids

A fluid at rest cannot carry shear, so every force it exerts is perpendicular to the surface it touches. From that one fact follows the pressure at any depth, the reading of every manometer, and the thrust on every gate, wall and dam you will ever design.

2.1 The Rules of Fluid Statics

The general rules of statics you learned for solids apply unchanged to fluids at rest. What changes is what a fluid can and cannot carry. From Chapter 1 we know two things, and everything in this chapter is built on them:

The two starting rules
  • A static fluid can have no shearing force acting on it.
  • Therefore any force between the fluid and a boundary must act at right angles to that boundary.

The second statement holds for curved surfaces too: at every point the force is normal to the surface at that point, so its direction changes as you move along the surface. That is the whole reason §2.9 has to treat curved surfaces separately from flat ones.

It also holds for any imaginary plane drawn inside a static fluid. We use that constantly: we cut an element of fluid out of the body with imaginary planes, draw the forces on its faces, and apply equilibrium.

Two consequences of equilibrium complete the toolkit:

  • For an element of fluid at rest, the sum of the components of force in any direction is zero.
  • The sum of the moments of those forces about any point is also zero.

In practice we test equilibrium by resolving along three mutually perpendicular axes and taking moments in three mutually perpendicular planes, setting each to zero. Everything that follows is that procedure applied to a cleverly chosen element.

2.2 Pressure, and Pascal’s Law

A fluid exerts a normal force on every boundary it touches. Those boundaries may be large, and the force may differ from place to place, so it is convenient to work not with force but with force per unit area — pressure:

p = FA (2.1)

If the force on each unit of area is the same, the pressure is said to be uniform. Units: N/m2, which is the pascal (Pa); dimensions M L−1 T−2, as Table 1.2 of Chapter 1 says. The bar is a convenient multiple: 1 bar = 105 N/m2 = 100 kPa.

Pressure is a scalar; force is a vector

This distinction is worth fixing now, because §2.6 depends on it. Pressure at a point has a magnitude but no direction — it is the same in all directions, as we are about to prove. Force has magnitude and direction, and its direction is set by the orientation of the surface it acts on. Two forces of equal size on differently oriented surfaces do not add to twice as much.

Pascal’s law: pressure at a point acts equally in all directions

Take a small element of fluid in the form of a triangular prism containing a point P. Let px act on the vertical face, py on the horizontal face, and ps on the sloping face, which makes an angle θ with the horizontal. The prism has depth δz into the page.

Summing forces in the x direction. The vertical face has area δy δz, and the sloping face has area δs δz, of which the component acting in the x direction is obtained by multiplying by sinθ = δy/δs. There is no x component from py. For equilibrium:

(2.2) px δy δz − ps δs δz δyδs = 0 px δy δz = ps δy δz  ⇒  px = ps

Summing forces in the y direction. The horizontal face has area δx δz, the y component of the sloping face is obtained with cosθ = δx/δs, and this time there is also the weight of the element, ρg × ½δx δy δz, acting downward:

(2.3) py δx δz − ps δx δz − ρg 12δx δy δz = 0 py = ps + 12ρg δy

Now shrink the element. As δy → 0 the weight term vanishes — it contains a product of three small lengths while the pressure terms contain only two — so it is negligible, and

Pascal’s law
px = py = ps

The element was so small that it can be regarded as a point, and θ could have been any angle in any orientation. Therefore the pressure at any point in a static fluid is the same in all directions. The proof extends to the z axis in exactly the same way.

This is why pressure is a scalar. It is also why a pressure gauge gives the same reading whichever way round you screw it into the pipe, and why the pressure just inside a tank wall does not depend on how the wall happens to be tilted.

2.3 How Pressure Varies with Depth

Vertically: pressure increases with depth

Consider a vertical column of fluid of constant cross-sectional area A, surrounded by the same fluid of density ρ. Its bottom face is at level z1 where the pressure is p1, and its top face at z2 where the pressure is p2.

Taking upward as positive, the forces on the vertical element are p1A up, p2A down, and the weight mg = ρgA(z2 − z1) down. In equilibrium:

(2.4) p1A − p2A − ρgA(z2 − z1) = 0 p2 − p1 = −ρg(z2 − z1)

The minus sign says what your intuition already knows: in a fluid under gravity, pressure decreases as height increases. Go deeper and it rises.

Horizontally: pressure is constant at the same level

Now take a horizontal cylinder of the same fluid, cross-section A, with pressure pl on the left end and pr on the right. Its weight acts vertically and so has no component along the axis. Summing forces in the x direction:

plA = prA  ⇒  pl = pr (2.5)
The rule you will use most in this course

Pressure in the horizontal direction is constant, throughout any one continuous fluid. “Continuous” is the word that does the work: the two points need not be connected by a straight pipe, or even be in the same vessel — they only need to have unbroken fluid between them. This is what makes every manometer in §2.5 readable.

The general case: any orientation

The two results above are special cases of one equation. Take a cylindrical element of length δs and area A, inclined at an angle θ to the vertical. Resolving along its own axis, with the weight ρgA δs contributing ρgA δs cosθ:

(2.6) pA − (p + δp)A − ρgA δs cosθ = 0 dpds = −ρg cosθ

Put θ = 90° and s lies horizontally, giving dp/dx = dp/dy = 0 — pressure is constant on any horizontal plane. Put θ = 0 and s is vertical, giving

dpdz = −ρg (2.7)

which is equation (2.4) again. Both earlier results drop out of one general statement, and that statement — equation (2.7) — is what the rest of the chapter integrates.

2.4 Pressure and Head; Gauge and Absolute

In a static fluid of constant density, dp/dz = −ρg integrates immediately to

p = −ρgz + constant

In a liquid with a free surface it is more natural to measure downward. Writing h for the depth below the free surface, so that z = −h:

p = ρgh + psurface (2.8)

At the surface of the fluids we deal with, that surface pressure is atmospheric. Since we live under the atmosphere and everything around us is already loaded by it, it is convenient to take atmospheric pressure as the datum and quote pressures above or below it.

Gauge and absolute
pgauge = ρgh

The lower limit of any pressure is zero — the pressure in a perfect vacuum. Measured from that datum instead, a pressure is called absolute:

pabs = ρgh + patm = pgauge + patm

Standard atmospheric pressure is 101.325 kPa, which is 1.013 bar, or 10.33 m of water, or 760 mm of mercury.

Pressure expressed as a head

Since g is essentially constant, any gauge pressure can be stated as the vertical height of a column of fluid of density ρ that would produce it. That height is called the head of fluid:

h = pρg = pγ (2.9)
A head is meaningless without its fluid

If a pressure is quoted as a head, the density of the fluid must be given too. Five metres of water and five metres of mercury are not remotely the same pressure — they differ by a factor of 13.6. “5 m head” on its own is not an answer.

Head is the natural currency of irrigation and water supply, because it is what you actually measure on site: the height of the reservoir above the field, the rise of water in a standpipe, the level in a piezometer. Chapters 3 to 5 will work almost entirely in metres of water for this reason.

2.5 Manometers

The relationship between pressure and head is not just a way of quoting numbers; it is a way of measuring them. An instrument that measures pressure by the height of a liquid column is a manometer, or liquid gauge.

The piezometer tube

The simplest manometer is a vertical tube, open at the top, attached to the vessel whose pressure is to be measured. Because the tube is open to the atmosphere, what it reads is a gauge pressure.

For the piezometer, the pressure at any tapping is simply the weight of the column standing above it:

pA = ρgh1      pB = ρgh2

Its limitations are severe, and they are worth stating because they motivate everything that follows. It works only for liquids, never for a gas, which would simply escape. And the height must be convenient: too small and the change cannot be detected, too large and the tube becomes absurd. A pressure of 2 bar would need a piezometer more than 20 m tall. Recall also from Chapter 1 §1.11 that a tube narrower than about 10 mm adds a capillary error of several millimetres.

The U-tube manometer

Bending the tube into a U and filling the bend with a second liquid — the manometric fluid — solves both problems at once, and allows gases to be measured as well as liquids. The manometric fluid must be denser than the fluid being measured, and the two must be immiscible, so that a clean interface forms.

The analysis uses equation (2.5) — pressure is the same at the same level in a continuous fluid. Points B and C lie on the same horizontal level in the continuous mercury, so

(2.10) pB = pC left arm:   pB = pA + ρgh1 right arm:   pC = patm + ρmangh2 ∴   pA = ρmangh2 − ρgh1   (gauge)

If the fluid being measured is a gas, its density is negligible beside that of the manometric fluid, ρman ≫ ρ, and the second term can be dropped:

pA = ρmangh2 (2.11)
How to read any manometer, without memorising a formula

Start at one end where the pressure is known. Walk along the fluid to the other end, and at each step: add ρgh when you go down, subtract ρgh when you go up, using the density of whichever fluid you are walking through. Change nothing when you move horizontally within one continuous fluid. When you arrive, set the total equal to the pressure at that end. Every manometer equation in this section can be written that way, and it will not let you down on an unfamiliar arrangement.

Measuring a pressure difference

Connect the two ends of a U-tube to two points on a pressurised system instead of leaving one open, and the instrument measures the difference between them — which is what you actually want when sizing a pipe or reading a flow meter in Chapter 3.

Equating the pressures at C and D and working down each leg gives

(2.12) pA + ρgha = pB + ρg(hb − h) + ρmangh pA − pB = ρg(hb − ha) + (ρman − ρ)gh

Again, if the fluid is a gas the ρ terms drop out and the result collapses to the very convenient pA − pB = ρmangh.

Making the instrument better

A plain U-tube has a nuisance: both levels move, so two readings must be taken at the same instant. Make the cross-section of one limb much larger — diameter D against d — and the large side barely moves while the small side moves a lot. The volume transferred is the same on both sides, so the fall on the wide side is z1 = z2(d/D)2, and

(2.13) p1 − p2 = ρgz2 [ 1 + (d/D)2 ] and if D ≫ d  ⇒  p1 − p2 ≈ ρgz2

so one reading suffices. If instead the pressure to be measured is very small — the few hundred pascals across a ventilation duct or a grain-dryer bed, say — tilt the narrow limb. The liquid then has to travel a long distance x along the tube to rise a short vertical distance z2, and the scale is stretched:

p1 − p2 = ρgz2 = ρgx sinθ (2.14)

Choosing a manometer

When connecting a manometer to a vessel, make sure there is no burr around the tapping: a burr disturbs the flow past it and creates a local pressure change that corrupts the reading. Beyond that, the instrument has a clear set of strengths and weaknesses.

In its favour

Very simple, with nothing to go wrong. No calibration is required — the pressure follows from first principles and a ruler. Cheap, and easy to improvise in the field.

Against it

Slow to respond, so it is useless for a fluctuating pressure. The plain U-tube needs two simultaneous readings. Small variations are hard to see. It cannot reach very high pressures unless several are connected in series. And for accurate work the temperature, and the temperature dependence of ρ, must be known.

2.6 Forces on Submerged Plane Surfaces

We now have everything we need to answer the question this chapter exists for: what force does the water put on that gate, and where does it act? The four facts we carry forward are the hydrostatic pressure distribution, equal pressure at equal depths, Pascal’s law, and forces normal to the boundary.

Pressure acting on a small area δA exerts a force F = p δA, at right angles to the surface. For a plane surface all these elemental forces are parallel, so they sum arithmetically to a single resultant force R, acting normal to the plane through a point called the centre of pressure.

The easy case first

For a horizontal plane submerged in a liquid, every point is at the same depth, so the pressure is uniform over the whole surface and the resultant is simply

R = pA = ρghA

acting at the centroid. The floor of a tank, the base of a farm pond, the horizontal cover of a sump — all of these are done in one line.

A plane surface at any angle

Now take a plane surface totally submerged in a liquid of density ρ and inclined at an angle θ to the horizontal. Measure z downward from the free surface, and measure s along the plane from the point O where the plane, produced, would cut the free surface. The two are related by z = s sinθ.

Taking the pressure as zero at the free surface, the pressure on an element at depth z is p = ρgz, so the force on that element is ρgz δA. Summing over the whole area, with ρ and g constant:

R = ρg Σ z δA

The term Σz δA is the first moment of area of the plane about the line where the plane meets the free surface, and by the definition of a centroid it equals Az̄. Therefore

The resultant force on a plane surface
(2.15) R = ρgAz̄ = γAz̄ = ρgAx̄ sinθ

In words: the resultant force equals the pressure at the centroid multiplied by the area. It acts at right angles to the plane. Note that the answer does not care what shape the surface is, only its area and the depth of its centroid.

The most common mistake in this chapter

The resultant force is found from the depth of the centroid, but it does not act there. It acts at the centre of pressure, which is always deeper, because the pressure on the lower half of the surface is greater than on the upper half. Getting R right and then applying it at G will give you the correct force and the wrong moment — and it is the moment that overturns a wall or jams a gate.

2.7 The Centre of Pressure

To locate C we use the principle that the moment of the resultant about any point equals the sum of the moments of all the elemental forces about that same point. It is convenient to take moments about O, the point where the plane produced meets the free surface, and to work in distances s measured along the plane.

The force on an element is ρgz δA = ρgs sinθ δA, and its moment arm about O is s. So the sum of the moments is

ρg sinθ Σ s2δA

The resultant R = ρgAx̄ sinθ acts at a distance Sc from O along the plane, so its moment is ρgAx̄ sinθ Sc. Equating and cancelling ρg sinθ:

Sc = Σ s2δAAx̄ = IoAx̄ (2.16)

because the summation Σs2δA is precisely the second moment of area Io of the plane about the axis through O. The depth of the centre of pressure below the free surface is then

D = Sc sinθ (2.17)

In words, and worth remembering in this form:

Sc = 2nd moment of area about a line through O1st moment of area about a line through O

Getting Io: the parallel axis theorem

Tables give the second moment of area about an axis through the centroid, not through O. The parallel axis theorem bridges the gap:

Io = IGG + Ax̄2 (2.18)

Substituting into (2.16) and (2.17) gives the two formulae you will actually use:

The centre of pressure
(2.19) Sc = IGGAx̄ + x̄ D = sinθ  ( IGGAx̄ + x̄ )

The first term is always positive, so Sc > x̄ always: the centre of pressure is always below the centroid. And it always will be, because IGG/Ax̄ shrinks as x̄ grows — which is why a deeply submerged gate has its centre of pressure almost exactly at its centroid, while a gate breaking the surface has it a third of the way up from the bottom.

If the surface is symmetrical about a vertical line, the centre of pressure lies on that line of symmetry and there is nothing more to do — which covers rectangular gates, circular covers and most of what you will design. If it is not symmetrical, the lateral position is found by taking moments about the line OG in exactly the same way, giving

d = Σ δA zxAz̄ (2.20)

2.8 Pressure Diagrams

For a vertical wall of constant width — a canal side, a tank wall, the upstream face of a small dam — there is a faster route that also gives a much better feel for what is happening. Plot the gauge pressure against depth. Since p = ρgz, the plot is a straight line running from zero at the surface to ρgH at the base: a triangle.

The area of the triangle is the resultant force per unit width of wall, in N/m:

R = 12 × H × ρgH = 12ρgH2 (2.21)

and it acts horizontally through the centroid of the diagram, which for a triangle is at H/3 above the base, that is at a depth

D = 23H (2.22)

Checking it against the moments method

The two methods must agree, and it is worth seeing that they do. Using (2.15) with a wall of unit width, A = H × 1 and z̄ = H/2:

R = ρg(H × 1)(H/2) = ½ρgH2  ✓

and using (2.19) with θ = 90°, IGG = 1 × H3/12 and x̄ = H/2:

D = H3/12H × H/2 + H2 = H6 + H2 = 2H3  ✓
When the diagram earns its keep

The pressure-diagram method comes into its own when a tank holds two liquids — oil floating on water is the standard case, and so is a layer of settled slurry under clear liquid. The diagram is then a triangle on top of a trapezium: the upper liquid produces a triangle down to the interface, and below it the lower liquid adds its own triangle on top of the constant pressure already inherited from above. Find the area and the centroid of each part separately, then combine the two forces by taking moments. Trying to do the same job with equation (2.19) is possible but far more error-prone.

For non-rectangular or non-vertical planes, go back to the moments method — drawing the diagram is then more trouble than it saves.

2.9 Forces on Submerged Curved Surfaces

On a curved surface the elemental forces are still normal to the surface, but the surface normal changes direction from point to point, so they are not parallel and cannot simply be added. The practical route is to compute the horizontal and vertical components separately and then combine them as vectors.

Case 1: fluid resting above the curved surface

Isolate the wedge of fluid ABC bounded by the curved surface AB, a vertical plane AC and a horizontal plane CB. The fluid in it is at rest, so it is in equilibrium.

Horizontally, no force can act on CB, because CB is horizontal and a static fluid exerts no shear. So the only horizontal forces are those on AC and on the curved face. They must balance:

Horizontal component

RH = the resultant force on the projection of the curved surface onto a vertical plane, computed exactly as in §2.6–2.8, and acting horizontally through the centre of pressure of that projection.

Vertically, there are no shear forces on the vertical edges, so the only vertical force available is the weight of the fluid in the wedge:

Vertical component

RV = the weight of the fluid directly above the curved surface, acting vertically downward through the centre of gravity of that volume.

Case 2: fluid below the curved surface

This is the case of a curved sluice gate, a radial (Tainter) gate, or the underside of a submerged pipe — and it is the one that catches people out. The horizontal component is found in exactly the same way as before. For the vertical component, imagine removing the curved surface and filling the space it occupied with fluid: the whole system would then be in equilibrium. So the force the surface must supply to maintain equilibrium is equal to the weight of the fluid that would have been there.

Fluid below: the vertical component acts upward

RV = the weight of the imaginary volume of fluid vertically above the curved surface, up to the level of the free surface — and it acts upward. Forgetting the word “imaginary”, or getting the direction wrong, is the classic error here. If in doubt, ask yourself which way the water is trying to push the gate: it is trying to lift a radial gate, not press it down.

Combining the components

(2.23) R = √RH2 + RV2 θ = tan−1 ( RV / RH )

The resultant acts through the point O where the horizontal line of action of RH crosses the vertical line of action of RV, at the angle θ to the horizontal.

A shortcut for circular surfaces — beyond the reference notes

If the curved surface is part of a circle, every elemental pressure force is normal to the surface and therefore points along a radius — straight at the centre of curvature. A set of forces that all pass through one point has a resultant that passes through that point too.

So for any circular gate, the resultant force passes through the centre of curvature, and you get its line of action free, without computing where RH and RV act. This is exactly why a radial gate is built as an arc with its hinge at the centre: the whole water thrust passes through the hinge pin, so it exerts no moment about the hinge, and the gate can be lifted against its own weight alone. It is a lovely piece of engineering, and it falls straight out of “pressure acts normal to the surface”.

2.10 Beyond the Core — Buoyancy and Modern Gauges

Extension material

The four topics in this section are not in the reference notes for this chapter and are outside the examinable core of ASE 221. They are here because each follows directly from what you have just proved, and because you will meet all four in practice: buoyancy every time something floats or a hydrometer is dipped, the paradox every time someone argues about tank shape, and the modern gauges on every pump station built in the last forty years.

Buoyancy and Archimedes’ principle

Section 2.9 gives this away almost for free. Take a body fully submerged in a liquid. The vertical force on its lower surface is the weight of the imaginary fluid column above that surface, acting upward. The vertical force on its upper surface is the weight of the real fluid above it, acting downward. The difference between the two columns is exactly the volume of the body. So the net vertical force is

FB = ρgV (2.24)

where V is the volume of fluid displaced. The horizontal forces cancel, because the vertical projection of the left half of the body is identical to that of the right half. This is Archimedes’ principle: the upthrust on a body equals the weight of the fluid it displaces, and it acts upward through the centroid of the displaced volume, a point called the centre of buoyancy.

For a floating body, equilibrium requires upthrust = weight, so ρgVdisplaced = mg, and the depth to which it sinks — the draught — follows immediately. Two consequences worth carrying away:

  • A hydrometer works because of this. The instrument of Chapter 1 §1.9 is a weighted float of fixed weight. In a denser liquid it needs to displace less volume, so it floats higher; the scale on its stem is simply a relative-density scale read backwards. Dipping a hydrometer into milk, latex or molasses is a direct measurement of ρ.
  • Draught depends on the water, not just the load. A pontoon or a pump barge floats deeper in fresh water than in seawater, because fresh water is less dense and so more of it must be pushed aside. Example 2.6 puts a number on it.

The hydrostatic paradox

Equation (2.8) says the pressure at a depth depends on the depth and nothing else — not on the shape of the vessel, and not on how much liquid it holds. Take three vessels with the same base area of 0.5 m2 and the same water depth of 1.2 m: one a straight cylinder, one flaring outward, one narrowing to a thin neck. The force on each base is identical:

F = ρghA = 1000 × 9.81 × 1.2 × 0.5 = 5886 N = 5.89 kN

even though the flared vessel might hold five times the weight of water of the narrow-necked one. This is the hydrostatic paradox, and it is not a paradox at all once you remember that the sloping walls also carry a vertical component of force — downward on a vessel that narrows upward, upward on one that flares. The vertical forces on the walls make up the difference exactly, which is §2.9 doing its job.

The practical point for you: the pressure rating of a tank, a pipe or a valve is set by the head above it, never by the volume behind it. A 200 mm delivery pipe from a header tank 80 m up the hill must be rated for 8 bar even if the tank holds only a few cubic metres.

The barometer

Invert a tube full of mercury into a dish of mercury and the column falls until its weight balances the atmosphere pushing on the dish, leaving a near-vacuum above it. The column height is then a direct reading of absolute atmospheric pressure:

patm = ρHggh = 13 600 × 9.81 × 0.760 = 101.4 kPa

Mercury is used rather than water for an obvious reason: a water barometer would need to be more than 10 m tall. Atmospheric pressure falls by roughly 1.2 kPa per 100 m of elevation, which matters for pump suction on a hillside scheme — as Problem 11 of Chapter 1 showed.

What is actually used on site

Manometers survive in the laboratory and in calibration work, but almost no field installation uses one. Two instruments have replaced them.

The Bourdon gauge

A flattened tube coiled into an arc straightens very slightly as the pressure inside it rises; a linkage multiplies that movement onto a dial. Rugged, cheap, reads instantly, and needs no liquid — but it is a mechanical instrument and it does need calibration, and it drifts. Always check whether the dial is marked in bar, kgf/cm2 or psi before you believe it, and note that it reads gauge pressure.

The pressure transducer

A diaphragm deflects under pressure and a strain gauge or piezoresistive element turns that deflection into a voltage or a 4–20 mA current. Fast enough to follow a transient, loggable, and capable of the accuracy a manometer gives without the fragility. This is what measures the pressure at a modern pump station, and what your year-2 laboratory work will use.

What none of this changes is the physics. Both instruments are calibrated, directly or indirectly, against a column of liquid of known density — which is to say, against equation (2.8).

2.11 Worked Examples

Example 2.1 — Pressure, head, and the units you will be handed

A pressure gauge on an irrigation main reads 500 kPa. Express this (a) as a head of water, (b) as a head of mercury, (c) in bar, kgf/cm2 and psi, and (d) as an absolute pressure. Take ρwater = 1000 kg/m3, ρHg = 13 600 kg/m3.

Step 1 — Head of water

h = pρg = 500 × 1031000 × 9.81 = 50.97 m of water

h = 51.0 m of water

Step 2 — Head of mercury

h = 500 × 10313 600 × 9.81 = 3.75 m of mercury

The same pressure, 13.6 times shorter a column. This is exactly why a manometer measuring a large pressure uses mercury and not water.

Step 3 — The practical units

From Table 2.1:

500 kPa ÷ 100 = 5.00 bar  ·  500 ÷ 98.07 = 5.10 kgf/cm2  ·  500 ÷ 6.895 = 72.5 psi

Note how close 5.00 bar and 5.10 kgf/cm2 are. The 2 % difference rarely matters for a first estimate, but it is a real difference and it is worth knowing which one the gauge in front of you is showing.

Step 4 — Absolute pressure

pabs = 500 + 101.3 = 601.3 kPa absolute

601 kPa absolute, or 6.01 bar (a)

Step 5 — What it means for the pipe

A static head of 51 m is what a PN 6 pipe (6 bar) would only just survive, with no allowance for surge. Chapter 1 §1.11 warned that closing a valve quickly on a long main can add several times the static pressure as a water-hammer wave, which is why PN 10 would be specified here in practice.

Example 2.2 — Reading a U-tube manometer

A mercury U-tube manometer is connected to a pipe carrying water. The mercury surface in the leg nearer the pipe stands 0.15 m below the pipe centreline, and the mercury in the open leg stands 0.40 m above that level. Find the gauge pressure of the water in the pipe, and express it as a head of water.

Step 1 — Identify the equal-pressure level

Let B be the mercury surface in the near leg, and C the point at the same level in the open leg. B and C lie on one horizontal plane in continuous mercury, so pB = pC. Everything follows from this one statement.

Step 2 — Walk down the left leg

Start at A in the water and go down 0.15 m to B, through water:

pB = pA + ρgh1 = pA + (1000)(9.81)(0.15) = pA + 1471.5

Step 3 — Walk down the right leg

Start at the open surface, where the gauge pressure is zero, and go down 0.40 m to C, through mercury:

pC = 0 + ρmangh2 = (13 600)(9.81)(0.40) = 53 366 Pa

Step 4 — Equate and solve

pA + 1471.5 = 53 366  ⇒  pA = 51 895 Pa

pA = 51.9 kPa gauge

Step 5 — As a head of water

h = 51 8951000 × 9.81 = 5.29 m of water

5.29 m of water

Check the sense of the answer: the mercury has been pushed 0.40 m up the open leg, which is 13.6 × 0.40 = 5.44 m of water; subtracting the 0.15 m of water in the other leg leaves 5.29 m. The arithmetic agrees, and the second route is a useful sanity check to run in your head.

Example 2.3 — Force and centre of pressure on a vertical canal gate

A rectangular sluice gate 1.5 m wide and 2.0 m high closes an opening in the vertical wall of an irrigation canal. Its top edge is 1.0 m below the water surface. Find the resultant force on the gate, the depth of the centre of pressure, and the moment about the hinge at the sill. Take ρ = 1000 kg/m3.

Step 1 — Geometry

A = 1.5 × 2.0 = 3.0 m2. The gate runs from 1.0 m to 3.0 m depth, so the centroid is at z̄ = 1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0 m. The gate is vertical, so θ = 90° and x̄ = z̄ = 2.0 m.

Step 2 — Resultant force

R = ρgAz̄ = (1000)(9.81)(3.0)(2.0) = 58 860 N

R = 58.9 kN, horizontal

Step 3 — Second moment of area

Rectangle, base b = 1.5 m, height d = 2.0 m:

IGG = bd312 = (1.5)(2.0)312 = 1.00 m4

Step 4 — Depth of the centre of pressure

D = IGGAx̄ + x̄ = 1.00(3.0)(2.0) + 2.0 = 0.167 + 2.0 = 2.167 m

D = 2.17 m below the free surface

That is 1.17 m below the top edge of the gate, i.e. 0.83 m above the sill — below the mid-height, as it must be.

Step 5 — Moment about the sill hinge

M = R × 0.833 = (58 860)(0.833) = 49 030 N·m

M = 49.0 kN·m

Had you carelessly applied R at the centroid instead, the lever arm would have been 1.0 m and the moment 58.9 kN·m — 20 % too high. The force would have been right and the hinge design wrong.

Example 2.4 — An inclined circular gate

A circular gate 0.8 m in diameter is set in a wall inclined at 60° to the horizontal. The centre of the gate is 2.5 m below the free surface. Find the resultant force and the depth of the centre of pressure.

Step 1 — Geometry

A = πR2 = π(0.4)2 = 0.5027 m2, z̄ = 2.5 m, and the distance along the plane from O is

x̄ = z̄sinθ = 2.5sin 60° = 2.50.8660 = 2.887 m

Step 2 — Resultant force

R = ρgAz̄ = (1000)(9.81)(0.5027)(2.5) = 12 328 N

R = 12.3 kN, normal to the gate

Note that the angle does not enter the force calculation at all — only the depth of the centroid does.

Step 3 — Centre of pressure

For a circle, IGG = πR4/4 = π(0.4)4/4 = 0.02011 m4. Then

Sc = 0.02011(0.5027)(2.887) + 2.887 = 0.0139 + 2.887 = 2.901 m
D = Sc sinθ = (2.901)(0.8660) = 2.512 m

D = 2.51 m below the free surface

Step 4 — Comment

The centre of pressure is only 12 mm below the centroid. That is the general pattern: the deeper and the smaller a gate, the more nearly uniform the pressure over it, and the closer C creeps to G. For a gate this size at this depth you could treat the load as uniform and be wrong by half a per cent — but you should know that you are approximating, and say so.

Example 2.5 — A curved surface: the fillet at the base of a tank

A water tank 2.4 m deep has its vertical wall joined to its floor by a quarter-circle fillet of radius 1.0 m. Find the horizontal and vertical components of the resultant force on the fillet, per metre width, and the magnitude and direction of the resultant.

Step 1 — Geometry

The fillet runs from B on the wall, at depth 2.4 − 1.0 = 1.4 m, to A on the floor at depth 2.4 m, a horizontal distance 1.0 m from the wall. The water lies above the curve, so this is Case 1 of §2.9.

Step 2 — Horizontal component

The vertical projection of AB is a rectangle 1.0 m tall and 1 m wide, running from depth 1.4 to 2.4 m, so A = 1.0 m2 and z̄ = 1.9 m:

RH = ρgAz̄ = (1000)(9.81)(1.0)(1.9) = 18 639 N

RH = 18.6 kN per metre width

acting at a depth D = (1×13/12)/(1.0×1.9) + 1.9 = 1.944 m.

Step 3 — Vertical component

This is the weight of the real water sitting directly above the curve. Over the 1.0 m horizontal span, that area is a 1.0 × 1.4 m rectangle plus the quarter circle:

Aabove = (1.0)(1.4) + π(1.0)24 = 1.400 + 0.785 = 2.185 m2
RV = ρg × Aabove × 1 = (1000)(9.81)(2.185) = 21 439 N

RV = 21.4 kN per metre width, downward

Step 4 — Resultant

R = √18.642 + 21.442 = √807.0 = 28.4 kN
θ = tan−1 21.4418.64 = 49.0° below the horizontal

R = 28.4 kN/m at 49.0° below the horizontal

Step 5 — Free check

The fillet is a circular arc, so by the shortcut in §2.9 the resultant must pass through the centre of curvature — the point 1.0 m out from the wall at depth 1.4 m. Drawing a line at 49.0° through that point locates the thrust on the structure without any further calculation, and gives you an independent check that the two components were combined the right way round.

Example 2.6 — A floating pump pontoon §2.10

A rectangular steel pontoon 4.0 m long, 2.0 m wide and 1.0 m deep carries a pump set; the whole assembly has a mass of 3000 kg. Find the draught in fresh water (ρ = 1000 kg/m3), and the change when it is moved to brackish water of ρ = 1025 kg/m3.

Step 1 — Equilibrium condition

A floating body displaces its own weight of fluid:

mg = ρgVdisp  ⇒  m = ρ (L × B × d)

Note that g cancels — the draught does not depend on gravity.

Step 2 — Draught in fresh water

d = mρLB = 3000(1000)(4.0)(2.0) = 0.375 m

d = 0.375 m, freeboard 0.625 m

Step 3 — Draught in brackish water

d = 3000(1025)(4.0)(2.0) = 0.366 m

d = 0.366 m — the pontoon rises 9 mm

Step 4 — Comment

Nine millimetres sounds trivial, and for a pontoon it is. It is not trivial for a hydrometer, which is built with a long thin stem precisely so that a small change in density produces a large change in draught. That is the same equation used as an instrument rather than as a check, and it is how the relative densities in Table 1.4 of Chapter 1 were measured in the first place.

2.14 Problems

Take g = 9.81 m/s2, ρwater = 1000 kg/m3 and ρHg = 13 600 kg/m3 unless told otherwise. Answers are given so that you can check yourself; the marks are in the working. Problems marked §2.10 draw on the extension material and are not examinable.

  1. A tank holds water to a depth of 3.5 m. Find the gauge pressure on the base in kPa, and express it as a head of mercury. 34.3 kPa; 0.258 m of mercury
  2. A gauge on a sealed vessel reads 350 kPa absolute at a site where atmospheric pressure is 101.3 kPa. Express the pressure as a gauge pressure in kPa and in bar, and as a head of water. 248.7 kPa gauge = 2.49 bar gauge; 25.35 m of water
  3. A mercury U-tube manometer is connected to a water pipe. The mercury surface in the near leg is 0.20 m below the pipe centreline, and the mercury in the open leg stands 0.35 m above that level. Find the gauge pressure in the pipe. 44.7 kPa
  4. An inclined manometer whose tube makes 15° with the horizontal contains oil of relative density 0.82. The oil moves 250 mm along the tube. Find the pressure difference, and state the reading the same pressure would give on a vertical manometer using the same oil. 521 Pa; only 64.7 mm vertically — a gain of 3.9×
  5. A vertical rectangular gate 2.0 m wide and 1.5 m high has its top edge at the water surface. Find the resultant force and the depth of the centre of pressure. 22.1 kN; D = 1.00 m
  6. The same gate is lowered so that its top edge is 2.0 m below the surface. Find the new resultant force and centre of pressure, and comment on how far C has moved relative to G. 80.9 kN; D = 2.82 m — C is now only 68 mm below G, against 250 mm before
  7. A canal wall 2.5 m high retains water to its top. Using the pressure-diagram method, find the resultant force per metre run and the height above the base at which it acts. 30.7 kN/m; 0.833 m above the base
  8. A circular inspection cover 1.0 m in diameter is set vertically in the wall of a tank with its centre 3.0 m below the water surface. Find the resultant force and the depth of the centre of pressure. 23.1 kN; D = 3.02 m
  9. A tank 3.0 m deep has a quarter-circle fillet of radius 1.5 m joining its vertical wall to its floor. Find RH, RV, and the magnitude and direction of the resultant, per metre width. 33.1 kN; 39.4 kN; R = 51.5 kN at 50.0° below the horizontal
  10. Three vessels of different shape — one straight-sided, one flaring outward, one narrowing to a neck — all have a base area of 0.5 m2 and hold water to a depth of 1.2 m. Find the force on each base, and explain in one sentence why they are equal even though the vessels hold very different weights of water. 5.89 kN on each — the sloping walls carry the difference as a vertical component
  11. §2.10 A rectangular pontoon 5.0 m × 2.5 m × 1.2 m deep, of total mass 4500 kg, floats in fresh water. Find its draught and freeboard. draught 0.360 m; freeboard 0.840 m
  12. §2.10 A cube of timber 0.3 m on a side, of relative density 0.65, floats in water. Find the depth to which it sinks, and the vertical force needed to hold it completely submerged. 0.195 m; 92.7 N
