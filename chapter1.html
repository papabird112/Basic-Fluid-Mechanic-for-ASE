1.1 What Fluid Mechanics Is For

Fluid mechanics is the branch of applied mechanics concerned with the statics and dynamics of fluids — both liquids and gases. It analyses their behaviour using the same fundamental laws you already know from solid mechanics: conservation of mass, of energy, and of momentum.

Why does an agricultural and biosystems engineer need it? Because almost everything the profession builds either moves water or is shaped by it:

  • Irrigation — canals, pipelines, sprinklers, drip emitters, pumps;
  • Drainage and flood protection — field drains, culverts, farm ponds, dykes;
  • Water supply and treatment for livestock housing and processing plants;
  • Processing lines — pumping milk, juice, purée, slurry and effluent;
  • Sprayers and applicators — nozzle pressure, droplet size, drift;
  • Air handling — grain dryers, ventilation in animal housing, pneumatic conveying.

Two things make fluid mechanics different from the solid mechanics you have studied. First, the nature of a fluid is genuinely different from that of a solid — that is §1.4. Second, in fluids we normally deal with a continuous stream with no beginning and no end, rather than with individual elements. That second difference is why Chapter 3 will teach you to draw a boundary in the flow, call it a control volume, and account for what crosses it.

1.2 The SI System of Units

A quantity can be expressed in whatever way you like, and over the years many ways have been used. Different countries still use different units for the same thing, and occasionally the same name for different things — an American pint is four-fifths of a British pint. To avoid all of that, this course uses the SI (metric) system throughout, and so should you.

Read this twice

It is essential that all quantities in an equation are expressed in the same system, or the answer will be wrong. Despite this warning, unit error remains the single most common mistake students make in this subject — more common than any misunderstanding of the physics.

Primary units

The SI system rests on six primary units, from which every other quantity can be built. Alongside each is its dimension — a statement of what kind of thing the unit measures, independent of the unit chosen. A metre, an inch, a mile and a kilometre are all lengths, so all have dimension L.

1.3 Dimensional Homogeneity

Any equation that describes a physical situation can only be true if both sides have the same dimensions. Such an equation is dimensionally homogeneous. This is not a formality; it is a free and very fast check on your own work.

Take the discharge over a rectangular weir, an equation you will derive in Chapter 3:

Q = 23 B √2g H3/2 (1.1)

The left-hand side is a discharge, dimension L3 T−1. On the right, B and H are lengths and g is an acceleration; the 2/3 is a pure number and carries no dimensions at all. So

L × (L T−2)1/2 × L3/2 = L1 + 1/2 + 3/2 T−1 = L3 T−1

Both sides agree, so the equation passes. Note that the check cannot confirm the 2/3 — dimensional analysis never determines a pure number. It can only tell you that the equation is not obviously broken.

The habit worth forming

Write the units beside every number as you substitute it, and carry them through the arithmetic. If your final line says

30 kg/(m·s) = 30 m

then you have certainly made a mistake, and you have found it in seconds rather than in the examination hall. Checking units will not tell you where the error is, but it will tell you with certainty that there is one.

Dimensional homogeneity has three uses. Two of them are yours from today: checking an equation, and converting between unit systems. The third — building dimensionless groups such as the Reynolds number, and using them to plan experiments and scale models — is the subject of dimensional analysis, and belongs to a later course. You will meet the first of those groups in Chapter 5.

1.4 What Is a Fluid?

We normally recognise three states of matter: solid, liquid and gas. But liquid and gas are both fluids, and mechanics sorts matter not by appearance but by how it responds to a shear stress — a force applied along a surface rather than perpendicular to it.

Definition

A fluid is a substance which deforms continuously, or flows, when subjected to a shearing force — no matter how small that force is.

The word that carries the weight is continuously. Push sideways on a rubber block and it distorts through some angle and holds there: the elastic stress inside has grown until it balances what you applied. Push sideways on the top of a layer of water and it never finds that balance. It keeps shearing for as long as you push, and stops only when you stop.

Fluids versus solids — the two differences

  1. For a solid, the strain is a function of the applied stress, provided the elastic limit has not been reached. For a fluid, the rate of strain is proportional to the applied stress.
  2. The strain in a solid is independent of how long the force is applied, and the deformation disappears when the force is removed. A fluid continues to flow for as long as the force is applied, and does not recover its original shape afterwards.

Classifying a substance is usually easy, but not always. Pitch and glass appear solid under their own weight, yet pitch will deform and spread out over days — rather than the fraction of a second water would take. And a solid past its elastic limit becomes plastic and seems to flow; it still fails the definition of a true fluid, because it flows only once a certain minimum shear stress is reached.

Two consequences you will use constantly

  • A fluid at rest carries no shear stress. If it did, by definition it would be moving. So every force between a static fluid and a boundary acts at right angles to that boundary. This one fact is what makes the whole of Chapter 2 tractable.
  • A fluid takes the shape of its container. That is why the shape of the vessel enters every calculation, and why §1.7 has to distinguish a liquid from a gas.

Shear in a moving fluid, and the no-slip condition

Shear stresses appear in a moving fluid whenever adjacent particles move at different velocities. If every particle moves at the same velocity there is no relative motion between them, and no shear stress is produced.

Consider water flowing along a pipe. At the wall the velocity is zero — a real fluid in contact with a solid boundary takes exactly the velocity of that boundary. This is the no-slip condition, and it is an experimental fact rather than something derived. The velocity then increases towards the centre of the pipe. That variation across the direction of flow is the velocity profile.

1.5 Newton’s Law of Viscosity

How do we turn those observations into a number? Consider a fluid element with a shearing force F acting on its top face of area A. The shear stress is force per unit area,

τ = FA (1.2)

The deformation this produces is measured by the angle φ, the shear strain. If a particle at E moves a distance x in time t, and sits a distance y from the fixed surface, then for a small deformation

(1.3) shear strain   φ = xy rate of shear strain   φt = xty = xt 1y = uy

because x/t is just u, the velocity of that particle. Experiment then shows that the shear stress is directly proportional to this rate of shear strain. Writing the ratio u/y in its differential form as the velocity gradient du/dy, and calling the constant of proportionality the dynamic viscosity μ:

τ = μ dudy (1.4)

This is Newton’s law of viscosity, and it is the single most-used equation of this chapter.

Rearranging (1.4) gives the units of viscosity: μ = τ ÷ (du/dy), so Pa ÷ s−1 = Pa·s, equivalently N·s/m2 or kg/(m·s); dimensions M L−1 T−1, exactly as Table 1.2 says. The older CGS unit, the poise (P), still appears on lubricant and food-industry data sheets: 1 Pa·s = 10 P = 1000 cP.

1.6 Newtonian and Non-Newtonian Fluids

A fluid that obeys equation (1.4) with μ constant is called Newtonian. Its shear stress is linearly dependent on the velocity gradient, and on a plot of τ against du/dy it is a straight line through the origin whose slope is the viscosity. Water, air, light oils, ethanol, glycerin and most petroleum products are Newtonian, and the whole of this course assumes it.

Fluids for which μ is not constant are non-Newtonian. Many of the materials a biosystems engineer actually pumps fall into this class.

  • Plastic: the shear stress must reach a certain minimum before flow begins at all.
  • Bingham plastic: the same yield stress τ0, but with n = 1 above it, so it then flows like a Newtonian fluid. Sewage sludge is the classic example, and so are drilling mud, chocolate and toothpaste. A pipeline carrying a Bingham material will not restart if the pressure gradient cannot exceed τ0 — a real failure mode in manure and slurry lines.
  • Pseudo-plastic: no minimum stress, but the viscosity decreases with rate of shear. Colloidal substances such as clay, milk and cement behave this way, and so do fruit purée and tomato paste. A pump handling purée meets a much thicker fluid at start-up than at running speed, which is why start-up torque, not running torque, sizes the motor.
  • Dilatant: the viscosity increases with rate of shear. Quicksand and concentrated starch suspensions. Rare industrially, and usually a nuisance when it appears.
  • Thixotropic: the viscosity decreases with the length of time the shear is applied — thixotropic jelly paints, yoghurt. Rheopectic substances do the opposite.
  • Viscoelastic: similar to Newtonian, but behaves like a plastic under a sudden large change in shear.

There is one more category, and it is not real: the ideal fluid, assumed to have no viscosity at all. No such substance exists, but it is a useful fiction — the Bernoulli equation of Chapter 3 is derived for it, and gives practically useful answers anyway.

Scope of this course

From Chapter 2 onward every fluid is Newtonian. If you go on to design a slurry, sludge or food-processing line, the equations of Chapter 5 need modification, because the Reynolds number and the friction factor are redefined for non-Newtonian materials. Know that the boundary exists, and know when you have crossed it.

1.7 Liquids and Gases

Liquids and gases are both fluids and share most of their behaviour, but they differ in two ways that matter for design.

Table 1.3. The two differences that matter.
LiquidGas
Compressibility Difficult to compress; usually treated as incompressible, with ρ constant. Easily compressed; changes volume with pressure, and is usually treated as compressible.
Volume and free surface A given mass occupies a given volume. Placed in a larger vessel it forms a free surface. No fixed volume. Expands to fill whatever vessel it is given, so no free surface forms.

The free surface is the more immediately useful of the two. Its existence is what allows a reservoir, a canal or a farm pond to be analysed at all, and what makes the manometers of Chapter 2 work. A gas has no such surface, which is why a manometer measuring gas pressure needs a liquid of its own inside it.

As for compressibility: we will treat water as incompressible everywhere in this course, and air too wherever it moves slowly. That assumption is examined more closely in §1.11.

1.8 Where Viscosity Comes From

Viscosity is the property that offers resistance to shear deformation. Different fluids deform at different rates under the same shear stress: syrup deforms more slowly than water. But the mechanism is not the same in a gas as in a liquid, and that difference produces opposite behaviour with temperature — a favourite examination question, and a genuine trap when you interpolate a table.

Viscosity in gases

The molecules of a gas are far apart and only weakly held in position by cohesion. As adjacent layers slide past each other there is a continuous exchange of molecules: molecules from a slower layer move into a faster one and cause a drag, while those moving the other way exert an accelerating force. It is this momentum exchange that produces the resistance.

Heat the gas and the molecular motion intensifies, so the exchange between layers increases — and the viscosity rises. Pressure also affects it, but under normal conditions the change is negligible in gases.

Viscosity in liquids

In a liquid there is some molecular interchange too, but the molecules are so much closer together that cohesive forces hold them in place far more rigidly, and it is cohesion that plays the leading role.

Heating a liquid reduces those cohesive forces (which reduces the shear stress) and increases the molecular interchange (which increases it). Because of this competition the temperature dependence takes a form such as

μT = μ0(1 + AT + BT2) (1.5)

where μT is the viscosity at T °C, μ0 the viscosity at 0 °C, and A and B are constants for that particular liquid. In practice the cohesive term wins, and the viscosity of a liquid falls as it is heated. High pressure raises the viscosity of a liquid, because relative movement of the molecules then needs more energy, but the effect is small over the range of this course.

The one sentence to remember

Heat a liquid and its viscosity falls. Heat a gas and its viscosity rises. The two run in opposite directions because the resistance has a different physical origin in each.

The effect is not small. Table 1.5 shows water at 60 °C is less than half as viscous as water at 20 °C — so a hot line has less than half the laminar head loss of a cold one, a result you will use in Chapter 5. This is why engineering tables list viscosity against temperature and say nothing about pressure.

1.9 Density, Specific Weight, Relative Density

The density of a substance is the quantity of matter contained in a unit volume. It can be expressed in three different ways, and students confuse them routinely. Be deliberate about which one you are holding.

Mass density

Mass density ρ (rho) is the mass of substance per unit volume:

ρ = mV (1.6)

Units kg/m3; dimensions M L−3. For water the value to remember is 1000 kg/m3.

Specific weight

Specific weight γ (gamma) is the weight per unit volume — the force exerted by gravity on a unit volume of the substance. By Newton’s second law, weight per unit volume = mass per unit volume × g, so

γ = ρg (1.7)

Units N/m3; dimensions M L−2 T−2. For water at 20 °C, γ = 998.2 × 9.81 ≈ 9.79 kN/m3. This is the constant that converts a head in metres into a pressure in pascals, so in Chapter 2 you will reach for it more often than for ρ itself.

Relative density

Relative density — also called specific gravity, SG — is the ratio of the mass density of a substance to a standard mass density. For solids and liquids that standard is the maximum density of water, which occurs at 4 °C at atmospheric pressure:

SG = ρsubstanceρwater at 4 °C (1.8)

It is a ratio of two like quantities, so it has no units and no dimensions — a pure number. That is exactly why it is the figure printed on drums, hydrometers and data sheets: it reads the same in every unit system. To recover a density in SI, multiply by 1000 kg/m3. Diesel at SG 0.85 is 850 kg/m3; mercury at SG 13.55 is 13 550 kg/m3.

Two traps worth naming

Relative density has no units. Specific weight has units of N/m3. They are different quantities with confusingly similar names, and a value of “0.85” on a supplier’s sheet is always the ratio, never γ.

Symbols vary between textbooks. These notes use γ for specific weight and SG for relative density. The reference notes for this course write them as ω and σ; other books use s or d. Always read the nomenclature list of whatever book you are in.

1.10 Dynamic and Kinematic Viscosity

The coefficient of dynamic viscosity

The coefficient of dynamic viscosity μ is defined as the shear force per unit area required to drag one layer of fluid, at unit velocity, past another layer a unit distance away. Working the definition through in units:

μ = forcearea ÷ velocitydistance = force × timearea = masslength × time

giving N·s/m2, or kg/(m·s). Water at 20 °C is 1.00 × 10−3 kg/(m·s) — almost exactly 1 cP, which makes the centipoise a convenient mental yardstick.

Kinematic viscosity

Viscous force resists motion; inertia sustains it. Which of the two dominates depends not on μ alone but on μ relative to ρ. That ratio appears so often that it is given its own name and symbol, the kinematic viscosity ν (nu):

ν = μρ (1.9)

Units m2/s; dimensions L2 T−1 — pure kinematics, no mass, which is where the name comes from. The CGS unit is the stokes (St), and lubricant grades are universally quoted in centistokes: 1 m2/s = 104 St = 106 cSt, so water at 20 °C is about 1 cSt.

Table 1.4 holds a result worth pausing on. Air has a dynamic viscosity roughly 55 times smaller than water, yet a kinematic viscosity about 15 times larger, because it is some 830 times less dense. It is ν that appears in the Reynolds number of Chapter 5, so air flowing at the same speed through the same duct is markedly more likely to be laminar than water is.

1.11 Further Properties — Beyond the Core

Extension material

The five topics in this section sit outside the examinable core of ASE 221. They are kept here because each one is needed somewhere in your degree — the ideal gas law in grain drying and ventilation, vapour pressure in pump selection, surface tension in soil water and spray application — and because it is better to meet them once now than to meet them for the first time when a design depends on them. Read this section for understanding; you will not be examined on it in this course.

The continuum assumption

A fluid is made of molecules with empty space between them. Air at room conditions holds about 2.5 × 1025 molecules per cubic metre, each travelling some 6 × 10−8 m between collisions. Strictly, “the density at a point” is meaningless. We ignore all of that: the continuum assumption treats the fluid as continuously distributed through space, so density, pressure and velocity are smooth functions of position and the tools of calculus apply. It holds whenever the molecular mean free path λ is tiny compared with the smallest length L of the problem — that is, whenever the Knudsen number Kn = λ/L is below about 0.01. It covers every pipe, channel and sprayer in this course, and fails only for rarefied flows such as high-vacuum systems.

The ideal gas law

For a liquid, density is nearly fixed and you look it up. For a gas it depends on both pressure and temperature, and you calculate it:

p = ρRT   or   ρ = pRT (1.10)

where R is the specific gas constant — the universal constant 8314 J/(kmol·K) divided by the molar mass. For dry air, R = 287 J/(kg·K). Both p and T must be absolute: pascals and kelvin. At 27 °C, using 27 in place of 300.15 K overstates the density elevenfold. Example 1.4 shows why this matters to a grain dryer.

Compressibility and bulk modulus

No fluid is truly incompressible. The bulk modulus K measures the pressure rise needed for a given fractional reduction in volume, K = −dp ÷ (dV/V), and has the units of pressure. For water at 20 °C, K ≈ 2.18 GPa — so compressing water by 1 % takes 21.8 MPa, about 218 atmospheres. That is the justification for treating liquids as incompressible everywhere else.

The stiffness does not vanish, though, and it reappears in one important place: the speed at which a pressure disturbance travels, c = √(K/ρ), which for water is about 1480 m/s. Because that speed is finite, closing a valve quickly on a long irrigation main launches a pressure wave that can exceed the pipe rating many times over. The phenomenon is called water hammer.

Vapour pressure and cavitation

Molecules escaping a free liquid surface exert a partial pressure called the vapour pressure pv, which depends only on temperature and is tabulated in the sixth column of Table 1.5. Boiling occurs wherever the local absolute pressure falls to pv — which can happen by heating at fixed pressure, or by dropping the pressure at fixed temperature.

The second route is the engineering problem. Where a flow accelerates — over a pump impeller vane, through a partly closed valve — the pressure falls, and if it reaches pv vapour bubbles form, are carried downstream and collapse violently enough to pit steel. This is cavitation. Its practical consequence is easy to state: the available lift is (patm − pv)/γ, which for water at 20 °C is 10.1 m but at 70 °C only 7.3 m. Hot water cannot be lifted as far as cold water. Your year-2 course turns this into the NPSH calculation.

Surface tension and capillarity

A molecule at a free surface has neighbours only below and beside it, so there is a net inward pull and the surface behaves like a stretched membrane. Surface tension σ is that tension per unit length, in N/m; for water against air at 20 °C it is 0.0728 N/m. For a spherical droplet of radius r the pressure difference across the surface is Δp = 2σ/r, so finer atomisation costs more pressure and more energy — the physics behind a spray nozzle’s pressure–droplet size relationship.

At a solid–liquid–gas junction, surface tension competes with adhesion to the solid. If the liquid wets the solid (water on clean glass, contact angle θ ≈ 0) it climbs the wall; if it does not (mercury on glass, θ ≈ 130°) it is pushed down.

Equating the vertical component of the tension around the wetted perimeter to the weight of the raised column gives

(1.11) σ(πd) cosθ = γ πd24 h h = 4σ cosθγd

The d in the denominator is the practical message: capillary error grows as the tube gets narrower. Example 1.5 shows why the manometer tubes of Chapter 2 should be at least 10 mm bore, and the same relation governs how water is held in the pores of a soil — the foundation of soil-water potential in irrigation design.

1.12 Worked Examples

Example 1.1 — Is the equation dimensionally homogeneous?

A student writes down the following expression for the head loss along a pipe, hf = 32μLu / (ρgd2), where L and d are lengths and u is a velocity. Check whether it can be correct.

Step 1 — Write down the dimension of every symbol

From Tables 1.1 and 1.2:

μ = M L−1 T−1, L = L, u = L T−1, ρ = M L−3, g = L T−2, d = L. The 32 is a pure number.

Step 2 — Numerator

(M L−1 T−1)(L)(L T−1) = M L T−2

Step 3 — Denominator

(M L−3)(L T−2)(L2) = M T−2

Step 4 — Divide

M L T−2 ÷ M T−2 = L

The right-hand side has dimension L

A head loss is a height, so the left-hand side is also L. The equation is dimensionally homogeneous and survives the check. (It is in fact the Hagen–Poiseuille result you will derive in Chapter 5.)

Step 5 — What the check did and did not prove

It proved that the equation is not obviously wrong. It could not confirm the 32, and it would not have caught an error such as writing d2 where the physics required 2d2. Dimensional homogeneity is a necessary condition for a correct equation, never a sufficient one.

Example 1.2 — Force and power to shear an oil film

A flat plate 0.8 m × 0.5 m slides at 0.9 m/s over a fixed surface, separated from it by a film of SAE 30 oil 1.2 mm thick, at 20 °C. Find the shear stress in the oil, the force needed to move the plate, and the power dissipated.

Step 1 — Assumptions and data

The gap is small and the plate large, so the velocity profile is linear (Figure 1.3) and edge effects are neglected. SAE 30 oil is Newtonian. From Table 1.4, μ = 0.29 Pa·s.

Step 2 — Rate of shear strain

dudy = Uh = 0.9 m/s0.0012 m = 750 s−1

Note the conversion of the gap to metres before dividing, and note that the metres cancel to leave s−1 — which is what Table 1.2 says a velocity gradient should be.

Step 3 — Shear stress from Newton’s law

τ = μ dudy = (0.29 Pa·s)(750 s−1) = 217.5 Pa

Step 4 — Force on the plate

A = 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.40 m2, so

F = τA = (217.5)(0.40) = 87.0 N

F = 87.0 N

Step 5 — Power dissipated

The plate moves at constant velocity, so all the work done against the viscous force ends up as heat in the oil:

P = FU = (87.0 N)(0.9 m/s) = 78.3 W

P = 78.3 W

Step 6 — Check

Halve the gap and both force and power double; run the same plate on water (μ = 0.001 Pa·s) and the force falls to 0.3 N. Both trends are right, and the second is why a journal bearing is filled with oil rather than water.

Example 1.3 — Density, specific weight and relative density

A tank holds 6.0 m3 of vegetable oil whose mass is 5220 kg. Find its density, specific weight and relative density, and the weight of oil in the tank.

Step 1 — Mass density

ρ = mV = 5220 kg6.0 m3 = 870 kg/m3

ρ = 870 kg/m³

Step 2 — Specific weight

γ = ρg = (870)(9.81) = 8535 N/m3 = 8.54 kN/m3

γ = 8.54 kN/m³

Step 3 — Relative density

SG = 8701000 = 0.87

SG = 0.87, dimensionless

This is the number that would be printed on the drum. Notice that it carries no units at all, so it would read 0.87 to an engineer working in any unit system on earth.

Step 4 — Weight of oil in the tank

Weight is a force, so it is either mg or γV — the same thing reached two ways, which is itself a useful check:

W = mg = (5220)(9.81) = 51 208 N   and   W = γV = (8535)(6.0) = 51 210 N

W ≈ 51.2 kN

Step 5 — The trap

A very common error is to answer “the weight is 5220 kg”. It is not. 5220 kg is the mass; the weight is 51.2 kN. Table 1.2 makes the distinction unambiguous: mass has dimension M, weight has dimension M L T−2.

Example 1.4 — Air density in a grain-drying plenum §1.11

A grain dryer draws ambient air at 35 °C and 101.3 kPa into a plenum of 12 m × 4 m × 2.5 m. (a) Find the mass of air in the plenum. (b) The heater then raises the air to 60 °C at essentially the same pressure. By what percentage does the mass flow delivered by the constant-speed fan fall?

Step 1 — Absolute temperature

T1 = 35 + 273.15 = 308.15 K    T2 = 60 + 273.15 = 333.15 K

Step 2 — Density before and after heating

ρ1 = 101 325(287)(308.15) = 1.146 kg/m3     ρ2 = 101 325(287)(333.15) = 1.060 kg/m3

Step 3 — Mass in the plenum

V = 12 × 4 × 2.5 = 120 m3

m = ρ1V = (1.146)(120) = 137.5 kg

m = 137.5 kg

Step 4 — Loss of mass flow

A fan is a volume machine: it moves the same m3/s regardless of density, so the mass flow falls in the same ratio as the density.

reduction = 1.146 − 1.0601.146 = 0.075 = 7.5 %

7.5 % less mass of air per second

Step 5 — Comment

Hot air carries more moisture per kilogram, so heating still helps. But the drying calculation must be done on a mass basis, and a fan curve quoted in m3/s does not tell you the mass flow until you supply the density.

Example 1.5 — Capillary error in a manometer tube §1.11

Water at 20 °C stands in a clean glass tube of 1.5 mm bore. (a) How far does capillarity raise the reading? (b) What bore is needed to keep the error below 3 mm?

Step 1 — Data

From Table 1.5 at 20 °C: σ = 0.0728 N/m, γ = 9.79 kN/m3. Clean glass and clean water wet completely, so θ ≈ 0 and cosθ = 1.

Step 2 — Rise in the 1.5 mm tube

h = 4σ cosθγd = 4(0.0728)(9790)(0.0015) = 0.0198 m

h = 19.8 mm

Almost 20 mm of pure instrument error — in a device whose whole purpose is to measure head to the millimetre.

Step 3 — Bore required for a 3 mm limit

d = 4σ cosθγh = 4(0.0728)(9790)(0.003) = 0.0099 m

d ≈ 10 mm

Step 4 — The design rule

This is the origin of the laboratory convention that piezometer and manometer tubes should be at least 10 mm bore. Below that, either enlarge the tube or apply a correction — and note that the correction depends on the cleanliness of the glass, which is why enlarging is preferred. You will meet these tubes for real in Chapter 2.

1.15 Problems

Take g = 9.81 m/s2 and use Tables 1.4 and 1.5 throughout. Answers are given so that you can check yourself; the marks are in the working. Problems marked §1.11 draw on the extension material and are not examinable.

  1. Express each of the following in SI base units: (a) a dynamic viscosity of 1 poise; (b) a kinematic viscosity of 1 stokes; (c) a specific weight of 9.79 kN/m3. 0.1 kg/(m·s); 1 × 10−4 m²/s; 9790 kg/(m²·s²)
  2. Show that the weir equation Q = (2/3)B√(2g)H3/2 is dimensionally homogeneous, where B and H are lengths. both sides L3 T−1
  3. Starting from τ = μ du/dy, derive the dimensions of dynamic viscosity, and confirm them against Table 1.2. M L−1 T−1
  4. A drum holds 6.0 m3 of vegetable oil of relative density 0.87. Find its mass, its weight, its density and its specific weight. 5220 kg; 51.2 kN; 870 kg/m³; 8.53 kN/m³
  5. A liquid has a dynamic viscosity of 0.048 Pa·s and a relative density of 0.913. Calculate its kinematic viscosity in m2/s and in centistokes. 5.26 × 10−5 m²/s = 52.6 cSt
  6. A plate of area 0.50 m2 slides at 0.60 m/s over a 0.80 mm film of oil, and the measured force is 45 N. Determine the shear stress and the dynamic viscosity of the oil. 90 Pa; 0.12 Pa·s
  7. Oil of dynamic viscosity 0.80 Pa·s fills the 25 mm gap between two large parallel plates. The upper plate moves at 1.2 m/s and the lower is fixed. Find the shear stress at the moving plate, at the fixed plate, and at mid-gap. Explain the result. 38.4 Pa at all three — the profile is linear, so du/dy is the same everywhere
  8. §1.11 A 0.50 m3 receiver holds compressed air at 900 kPa absolute and 27 °C. Find the density of the air and the mass stored. 10.45 kg/m³; 5.22 kg
  9. §1.11 What pressure increase is required to reduce the volume of water by 0.50 %? Take K = 2.18 GPa. Comment on whether treating water as incompressible was reasonable. 10.9 MPa ≈ 109 bar
  10. §1.11 Calculate the capillary rise of water at 20 °C in a clean glass tube of 3.0 mm bore, and state the result for the same tube if the water were replaced by mercury (σ = 0.484 N/m, θ = 130°, SG 13.55). +9.9 mm; −3.1 mm (a depression)
  11. §1.11 A pump handles water at 50 °C at a site where atmospheric pressure is 96.0 kPa (about 450 m elevation). Find the greatest theoretical suction lift, and compare it with the 10.1 m available for cold water at sea level. 8.6 m
