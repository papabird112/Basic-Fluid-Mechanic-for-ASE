1.1 What Is a Fluid?

Matter is usually sorted into solid, liquid and gas by how it looks. Mechanics sorts it by how it responds to a shear stress — a force applied along a surface rather than perpendicular to it.

Definition

A fluid is a substance that deforms continuously under an applied shear stress, no matter how small that stress is. A solid resists shear with a finite deformation and then stops.

The word that carries the weight is continuously. Push sideways on a rubber block and it distorts through some angle and holds there: the elastic stress inside has grown until it balances what you applied. Push sideways on the top of a layer of water and it never finds that balance. It keeps shearing for as long as you push, and stops only when you stop.

Two consequences follow immediately, and both are used constantly from Chapter 2 onward:

  • A fluid at rest carries no shear stress. If it did, by definition it would be moving. So the force on any surface in contact with a static fluid is purely normal to that surface. This is the single fact that makes hydrostatics tractable.
  • A fluid takes the shape of its container. A liquid has a definite volume and forms a free surface; a gas has neither, and expands to fill whatever it is given.

One more experimental fact belongs here, because it is not obvious and everything in Chapter 7 depends on it: the no-slip condition. A real fluid in contact with a solid boundary has exactly the velocity of that boundary — zero relative velocity at the wall. Dust on a fan blade stays there for the same reason a pipe wall drags on the water passing it.

1.2 The Continuum Assumption

A fluid is made of molecules with empty space between them. Air at room conditions contains about 2.5 × 1025 molecules per cubic metre, each travelling some 6 × 10−8 m between collisions. Strictly, “the density at a point” is meaningless: shrink the sampling volume far enough and the answer flickers between zero and the density of a single molecule.

We ignore all of that. The continuum assumption treats the fluid as a substance that is continuously distributed through space, so that properties such as density, pressure and velocity are smooth functions of position and time and the tools of calculus apply.

The assumption is valid whenever the smallest length scale of the problem is enormous compared with the distance a molecule travels between collisions (the mean free path, λ). The ratio is the Knudsen number:

Kn = λL (1.1)

For Kn < 0.01 — which covers every pipe, pump, channel and sprayer in this course — the continuum model is excellent. It fails for rarefied flows: upper-atmosphere aerodynamics, vacuum systems, and gas flow inside pores of a few hundred nanometres. We will not meet those, but it is worth knowing that the assumption is an assumption.

1.3 Density, Specific Weight, Specific Gravity

Three closely related quantities describe how much matter a fluid packs into a given volume. Students confuse them routinely, and the confusion is expensive, so be deliberate about which one you are holding.

Density

Density ρ (rho) is mass per unit volume:

ρ = mV (1.2)

SI units kg/m3; dimensions [M L−3]. For water the value to remember is 1000 kg/m3 — strictly that is the maximum, at 4 °C; at 20 °C it is 998.2 and at 60 °C it is 983.2. For rough work, 1000 is fine. For a pump NPSH calculation on hot water, it is not.

Specific weight

Specific weight γ (gamma) is weight per unit volume — density times gravitational acceleration:

γ = ρg (1.3)

SI units N/m3. For water at 20 °C, γ = 998.2 × 9.81 = 9793 N/m3 ≈ 9.79 kN/m3. This is the constant that converts a head in metres into a pressure in pascals, so you will use it more than ρ itself in Chapter 2.

Specific gravity

Specific gravity SG (also called relative density) is the density of the fluid divided by the density of a reference — water at 4 °C for liquids, air for gases:

SG = ρρwater (1.4)

SG is dimensionless, which is exactly why it is quoted on drums, data sheets and hydrometers: it reads the same in every unit system. To recover a density in SI, multiply by 1000 kg/m3. Diesel at SG 0.85 is 850 kg/m3; mercury at SG 13.55 is 13 550 kg/m3.

A trap worth naming

Specific gravity has no units. Specific weight has units of N/m3. They are different quantities with similar names, and a value of “0.85” for something called “specific …” on a supplier’s data sheet is always SG, never γ. Also, specific volume v = 1/ρ (m3/kg) is a fourth member of the family, common in thermodynamics and rare here.

1.4 Gases and the Ideal Gas Law

For a liquid, density is nearly fixed and you look it up. For a gas it is not: it depends on both pressure and temperature, and you calculate it. At the pressures and temperatures of this course, air obeys the ideal gas law to within a fraction of a percent:

p = ρRT   or   ρ = pRT (1.5)

where R is the specific gas constant for that gas — the universal constant 8314 J/(kmol·K) divided by the molar mass. For dry air, R = 287 J/(kg·K).

Absolute, always

Equation (1.5) requires absolute pressure in pascals and absolute temperature in kelvin. A gauge pressure or a temperature in °C substituted here is simply wrong, and the error is large: at 27 °C, using 27 instead of 300.15 K overstates the density elevenfold. Write T = t + 273.15 as a separate line every time.

Two everyday consequences. First, warm air is less dense than cool air at the same pressure — which is why a drying fan that is rated in m3/s delivers less mass of air once the heater comes on (Example 1.2). Second, air is about 830 times lighter than water, which is why the weight of the atmosphere in a room is usually neglected while the weight of water in a tank never is.

1.5 Viscosity

Density tells you how heavy a fluid is. Viscosity tells you how strongly it resists being sheared — how much stress it takes to make one layer slide over its neighbour. It is the property that produces friction in a pipe, drag on a body, and the power bill of every pump.

Consider the standard experiment. A fluid fills the gap h between two large parallel plates. The lower plate is fixed; the upper is dragged sideways at a steady velocity U by a force F.

Experiment gives a simple result: the force needed is proportional to the plate area and to the velocity, and inversely proportional to the gap. Writing the shear stress as τ = F/A:

τ = μ dudy (1.6)

This is Newton’s law of viscosity. The constant of proportionality μ (mu) is the dynamic or absolute viscosity. The gradient du/dy is the rate of shear strain — in the linear profile of Figure 1.2 it is simply U/h.

Rearranging (1.6) gives the units: μ = τ / (du/dy), so Pa ÷ s−1 = Pa·s, equivalently N·s/m2 or kg/(m·s); dimensions [M L−1 T−1]. The older CGS unit, the poise (P), still appears on lubricant and food-industry data sheets: 1 Pa·s = 10 P = 1000 cP. Water at 20 °C is almost exactly 1 cP, which makes the centipoise a convenient mental yardstick.

Where the resistance comes from

In a liquid, viscosity comes mainly from cohesive forces between molecules. Heat the liquid, the molecules separate, cohesion weakens — and viscosity falls. In a gas, the molecules are already far apart; resistance comes from momentum carried across the flow by random molecular motion. Heat the gas, that motion intensifies — and viscosity rises. The two behave in opposite directions, which is a favourite examination question and a genuine trap when you interpolate a table.

1.6 Newtonian and Non-Newtonian Fluids

A fluid for which μ in equation (1.6) is a constant — a straight line through the origin on a plot of τ against du/dy — is called Newtonian. Water, air, light oils, ethanol, glycerin and most petroleum products are Newtonian, and the whole of this course assumes it.

Many of the materials a biosystems engineer actually pumps are not. Their apparent viscosity depends on how hard they are being sheared, and sometimes on how long.

  • Shear-thinning (pseudoplastic): apparent viscosity falls as shear rate rises. Fruit purée, tomato paste, latex, blood, most polymer solutions. A pump handling purée sees a much thinner fluid at running speed than at start-up, which is why start-up torque, not running torque, sizes the motor.
  • Shear-thickening (dilatant): apparent viscosity rises with shear rate. Concentrated starch suspensions, wet sand. Rare industrially, and usually a nuisance when it appears.
  • Bingham plastic: behaves as a solid until the shear stress exceeds a yield stress τ0, then flows like a Newtonian fluid. Sewage sludge, drilling mud, chocolate, toothpaste. A pipeline carrying a Bingham material will not restart if the pressure gradient cannot exceed the yield stress — a real failure mode in manure and slurry lines.
  • Time-dependent behaviour is a further complication: thixotropic materials (yoghurt, some paints) thin with continued shearing at constant rate and recover when left to stand.
Scope of this course

From Chapter 2 onward every fluid is Newtonian. If you go on to design a slurry, sludge or food-processing line, the equations of Chapter 7 need modification — the Reynolds number and friction factor are redefined. Know that the boundary exists and where you crossed it.

1.7 Kinematic Viscosity

Viscous force resists motion; inertia sustains it. Which of the two dominates depends not on μ alone but on μ relative to ρ. That ratio appears so often that it is given its own name and symbol, the kinematic viscosity ν (nu):

ν = μρ (1.7)

Its units are m2/s, with dimensions [L2 T−1] — pure kinematics, no mass, which is where the name comes from. The CGS unit is the stokes (St), and lubricant grades are universally quoted in centistokes: 1 m2/s = 106 cSt, so water at 20 °C is about 1 cSt.

Table 1.1 holds a result worth pausing on. Air has a dynamic viscosity roughly 55 times smaller than water, yet a kinematic viscosity about 15 times larger, because it is some 830 times less dense. In the Reynolds number of Chapter 7 it is ν that appears, so air flowing at the same speed through the same duct is markedly more likely to be laminar than water is.

1.8 Viscosity and Temperature

Viscosity is strongly temperature-dependent and only weakly pressure-dependent, so in practice we tabulate against temperature and ignore pressure. The effect is not small: water at 60 °C is less than half as viscous as water at 20 °C, and a hot line therefore has less than half the laminar head loss of a cold one.

1.9 Compressibility and Bulk Modulus

No fluid is truly incompressible. The bulk modulus of elasticity K measures the pressure rise needed to produce a given fractional reduction in volume:

K = − dpdV/V (1.8)

The minus sign makes K positive, since a pressure increase produces a volume decrease. Because dV/V is dimensionless, K has the units of pressure. For water at 20 °C, K ≈ 2.18 GPa.

Put a number to that. Compressing water by 1 % requires

Δp = K × 0.01 = 2.18 × 109 × 0.01 = 21.8 MPa ≈ 218 bar

Some 218 atmospheres for a one per cent change. This is the justification for the assumption we make everywhere else: liquids are treated as incompressible, with ρ constant.

The compressibility does not vanish, though, and it reappears in one important place. The speed at which a pressure disturbance travels through a fluid — the speed of sound — is set by it:

c = √Kρ (1.9)

For water, c = √(2.18 × 109 / 998) ≈ 1480 m/s. That finite speed is what makes water hammer possible: close a valve on a long pipeline quickly and the deceleration of the column generates a pressure wave that can exceed the pipe’s rated pressure many times over. The fluid’s stiffness, which we spend the whole course ignoring, is exactly what makes the transient dangerous.

Gases are a different matter. Air at atmospheric pressure has K ≈ 0.14 MPa — about fifteen thousand times more compressible than water. A gas may still be treated as incompressible when it moves slowly (below roughly 30 % of its own speed of sound, about 100 m/s for air), which covers ventilation ducts, drying fans and pneumatic conveying at low pressure. Beyond that, density changes matter and the subject becomes compressible flow.

1.10 Vapour Pressure and Cavitation

Molecules at the free surface of a liquid escape into the space above it, and at equilibrium the partial pressure they exert is the vapour pressure pv. It depends only on temperature, and for water it is tabulated in the sixth column of Table 1.2.

Boiling occurs whenever the local absolute pressure falls to the vapour pressure. That can be reached in two ways: raise pv by heating at fixed pressure (a kettle), or drop the local pressure at fixed temperature. The second route is the engineering problem.

Cavitation

Where a flow accelerates — over a pump impeller vane, through a partly closed valve, past a propeller tip — the pressure falls. If it falls to pv, vapour bubbles form. Carried downstream into higher pressure, they collapse violently. The implosions are extremely localised and produce pressures high enough to pit steel. The symptoms are a gravel-rattling noise, loss of head and efficiency, vibration, and eventual destruction of the impeller.

Cavitation is a property problem, not a flow problem, which is why it belongs in this chapter. Its cure is to keep the pressure at the suction side of a pump above pv by a stated margin — the net positive suction head, treated in Chapter 8. What matters here is the chain of reasoning that sets the limit, and Example 1.4 works it through: hot water cannot be lifted as far as cold water, because its vapour pressure has already consumed part of the available atmospheric head.

1.11 Surface Tension and Capillarity

A molecule inside a liquid is pulled equally in all directions by its neighbours. A molecule at the surface has neighbours only below and beside it, so there is a net inward pull. The result is that the free surface behaves like a stretched membrane, in tension. Surface tension σ (sigma) is that tension per unit length of surface, in N/m. For water against air at 20 °C, σ = 0.0728 N/m.

Surface tension explains the spherical droplet from a sprayer nozzle, the insect standing on a pond, and the pressure inside a bubble. For a spherical droplet of radius r, balancing the force pulling the two halves apart against the tension around the circumference gives

Δp = 2σr (1.10)

Smaller droplets are at higher internal pressure — and, since the same tension resists their formation, finer atomisation costs more energy. This is the physics behind a spray nozzle’s pressure–droplet size relationship in pesticide application.

Capillarity

At a solid–liquid–gas junction, surface tension competes with adhesion to the solid. If the liquid wets the solid (water on clean glass, contact angle θ ≈ 0), the liquid climbs the wall and the meniscus is concave. If it does not (mercury on glass, θ ≈ 130°), the liquid is pushed down and the meniscus is convex.

Equate the vertical component of the tension around the wetted perimeter to the weight of the raised column:

(1.11) σ(πd) cosθ = γ πd24 h h = 4σ cosθγd

The d in the denominator is the practical message: capillary error grows as the tube gets narrower. Example 1.3 shows why a piezometer or manometer tube smaller than about 10 mm bore introduces a reading error you cannot ignore, and the same relation governs how water is held in the pores of a soil — the foundation of soil-water potential in irrigation design.

1.12 Worked Examples

Example 1.1 — Force and power to shear an oil film

A flat plate 0.8 m × 0.5 m slides at 0.9 m/s over a fixed surface, separated from it by a film of SAE 30 oil 1.2 mm thick. The oil is at 20 °C. Find the shear stress in the oil, the force needed to move the plate, and the power dissipated.

Step 1 — Assumptions and data

The gap is small and the plate large, so the velocity profile is linear (Figure 1.2) and the edges are neglected. SAE 30 oil is Newtonian. From Table 1.1, μ = 0.29 Pa·s.

Step 2 — Rate of shear strain

dudy = Uh = 0.90.0012 = 750 s−1

Note the conversion of the gap to metres before dividing. This is where the arithmetic usually goes wrong.

Step 3 — Shear stress from Newton’s law

τ = μ dudy = (0.29)(750) = 217.5 Pa

Step 4 — Force on the plate

A = 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.40 m2, so

F = τA = (217.5)(0.40) = 87.0 N

F = 87.0 N

Step 5 — Power dissipated

The plate moves at constant velocity, so all the work done against the viscous force ends up as heat in the oil:

P = FU = (87.0)(0.9) = 78.3 W

P = 78.3 W

Step 6 — Check

Halve the gap and both the force and the power double; run the same plate on water (μ = 0.001 Pa·s) and the force falls to 0.3 N. Both trends are right, and the second is why a journal bearing is filled with oil rather than water.

Example 1.2 — Air density in a grain-drying plenum

A grain dryer draws ambient air at 35 °C and 101.3 kPa into a plenum of 12 m × 4 m × 2.5 m. (a) Find the mass of air in the plenum. (b) The heater then raises the air to 60 °C at essentially the same pressure. By what percentage does the mass flow delivered by the (constant-volume) fan fall?

Step 1 — Absolute temperature

T1 = 35 + 273.15 = 308.15 K    T2 = 60 + 273.15 = 333.15 K

Step 2 — Density before heating

ρ1 = pRT1 = 101 325(287)(308.15) = 1.146 kg/m3

Step 3 — Mass in the plenum

V = 12 × 4 × 2.5 = 120 m3

m = ρ1V = (1.146)(120) = 137.5 kg

m = 137.5 kg

Step 4 — Density after heating

ρ2 = 101 325(287)(333.15) = 1.060 kg/m3

Step 5 — Loss of mass flow

A fan is a volume machine: it moves the same m3/s regardless of density, so the mass flow falls in the same ratio as the density.

reduction = 1.146 − 1.0601.146 = 0.075 = 7.5 %

7.5 % less mass of air per second

Step 6 — Comment

Hot air carries more moisture per kilogram, so heating still helps. But the drying calculation must be done on a mass basis, and a fan curve quoted in m3/s does not tell you the mass flow until you supply the density. Note also that ρ ∝ 1/T only because the pressure was held constant.

Example 1.3 — Capillary error in a piezometer

Water at 20 °C stands in a clean glass piezometer tube of 1.5 mm bore. (a) How far does capillarity raise the reading? (b) What bore is needed to keep the error below 3 mm?

Step 1 — Data

From Table 1.2 at 20 °C: σ = 0.0728 N/m, γ = 9.79 kN/m3. Clean glass and clean water wet completely, so θ ≈ 0 and cosθ = 1.

Step 2 — Rise in the 1.5 mm tube

h = 4σ cosθγd = 4(0.0728)(1)(9790)(0.0015) = 0.0198 m

h = 19.8 mm

Almost 20 mm of pure instrument error — in a device whose whole purpose is to measure head to the millimetre.

Step 3 — Bore required for a 3 mm limit

Rearrange (1.11) for d:

d = 4σ cosθγh = 4(0.0728)(9790)(0.003) = 0.0099 m

d ≈ 10 mm

Step 4 — The design rule

This is the origin of the laboratory convention that piezometer and manometer tubes should be at least 10 mm bore. Below that, either enlarge the tube or apply a correction — and note that the correction depends on the cleanliness of the glass, which is why enlarging is preferred.

Example 1.4 — Why hot water cannot be lifted as far

A centrifugal pump at a processing plant draws water by suction lift from an open tank. Standard atmospheric pressure is 101.3 kPa. Find the greatest theoretical suction lift for water at (a) 20 °C and (b) 70 °C, and comment on the practical limit.

Step 1 — The physical limit

Atmospheric pressure on the tank surface is what pushes the water up the suction pipe. The lowest absolute pressure the pump inlet can ever reach is the vapour pressure — below that the water boils. So the available pressure difference is patm − pv, and the greatest height it can support is that difference divided by the specific weight.

zmax = patm − pvγ

Step 2 — Water at 20 °C

Table 1.2: pv = 2.34 kPa, γ = 9.79 kN/m3.

zmax = 101 325 − 23409790 = 10.1 m

zmax = 10.1 m

Step 3 — Water at 70 °C

Table 1.2: pv = 31.16 kPa, γ = 9.59 kN/m3.

zmax = 101 325 − 31 1609590 = 7.3 m

zmax = 7.3 m

Step 4 — The practical limit

Both figures are ceilings that no real installation reaches. Friction in the suction pipe, the velocity head at the inlet, and the pump’s own required NPSH all eat into the margin, and the site may be above sea level, which lowers patm as well. A common working rule is to design cold-water suction lifts to no more than 5–6 m, and to place a pump handling hot liquid below the source so that it runs with a flooded suction. The full NPSH calculation appears in Chapter 8; the reason for it is here.

1.14 Self-Check

Forty-two questions on the ideas of this chapter. Each is marked the moment you answer it, in your browser; nothing is stored and nothing is sent anywhere. Every question carries the section it came from, so a wrong answer tells you exactly what to re-read, and the summary at the end lists the sections to go back to.

Work through these before the written problems in §1.15. The point of this section is to check that you hold the concepts; the point of the next one is to check that you can carry a calculation through. Failing the second because of the first wastes an afternoon.

1.16 References

  2. Cengel, Y. A. and Cimbala, J. M. Fluid Mechanics: Fundamentals and Applications, 4th ed. McGraw-Hill. Chapter 2, “Properties of Fluids” — the clearest presentation of vapour pressure and cavitation for a first course.
  3. Munson, B. R., Young, D. F. and Okiishi, T. H. Fundamentals of Fluid Mechanics, 8th ed. Wiley. Chapter 1 — good on non-Newtonian behaviour and on the continuum assumption.
  4. Finnemore, E. J. and Franzini, J. B. Fluid Mechanics with Engineering Applications, 10th ed. McGraw-Hill. Written for civil and water-resources engineers; the property tables are in the units used on site.
  5. Rao, M. A. Rheology of Fluid, Semisolid, and Solid Foods, 3rd ed. Springer. The reference to reach for when a biosystems fluid turns out not to be Newtonian.
  6. ISO 3104 / ASTM D445 — Determination of kinematic viscosity of transparent and opaque liquids. The standard test behind every viscosity figure quoted on a data sheet.
