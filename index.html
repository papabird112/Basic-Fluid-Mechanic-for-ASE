Course Description

A fluid is any substance that cannot resist a shear stress without moving. That single sentence separates this subject from the mechanics you already know: a fluid at rest can carry pressure but not shear, and a fluid in motion deforms continuously for as long as the stress is applied. Almost everything in this course follows from taking that definition seriously.

The course is deliberately compact. It covers the material a biosystems or agricultural engineer actually uses — pressure and force on submerged surfaces, the three control-volume equations (mass, energy, momentum), the friction that dominates any real pipeline, and the pumps and meters that make an irrigation or processing system work — and stops there. Compressible flow, boundary-layer theory and computational methods are left to later electives.

Emphasis throughout is on getting a defensible number. Every chapter ends with worked examples carried through in SI units, with the assumptions stated and the answer checked for physical sense.

Learning Outcomes

On successful completion of this course a student will be able to:

  1. State the properties of a fluid — density, viscosity, bulk modulus, vapour pressure, surface tension — and obtain them from tables and from the ideal gas law.
  2. Determine the pressure at any point in a static fluid, read a manometer, and compute the resultant force and centre of pressure on a plane or curved submerged surface.
  3. Apply conservation of mass, energy and momentum to a chosen control volume, and defend the choice of that control volume.
  4. Use the Bernoulli equation within its limits, and recognise the situations where it fails.
  5. Classify a flow as laminar or turbulent, and compute head loss in a pipe using the Darcy–Weisbach equation and the Moody chart.
  6. Size a pipeline and select a pump by matching a system curve to a pump characteristic, checking NPSH against cavitation.
  7. Use dimensional analysis to plan an experiment and to scale model results to a prototype.

Course Structure

Part I — Fluids at Rest

Chapters 1–2. What a fluid is, the properties that describe it, hydrostatic pressure, manometry, forces on submerged surfaces and buoyancy.

Part II — Fluids in Motion

Chapters 3–6. Describing a flow field, the control volume, and the mass, energy and momentum equations; dimensional analysis and similitude.

Part III — Real Systems

Chapters 7–9. Viscous flow and head loss in pipes, pipe networks, pump selection and cavitation, and the measurement of pressure and flow rate.

Prerequisites

Engineering Mechanics (statics), Calculus II and Physics I. You will need to be comfortable resolving forces and taking moments, evaluating a definite integral, and working in consistent SI units. Differential equations are not required; the few that appear are separable and are solved in the text.

On units

The notes are written in SI throughout. Where industry practice still quotes a value in another unit — pump head in metres but pipe diameter in inches, pressure in bar or in kgf/cm² — the conversion is given at the point of use rather than banished to an appendix. Mixing units is the most common source of error in this subject, and it is worth a moment of care every time.
