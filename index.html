Course Description

A fluid is any substance that cannot resist a shear stress without moving. That single sentence separates this subject from the mechanics you already know: a fluid at rest can carry pressure but not shear, and a fluid in motion deforms continuously for as long as the stress is applied. Almost everything in this course follows from taking that definition seriously.

This is a first-year foundation course. Its job is to put the four tools you will use for the rest of the degree into your hands and make you fluent with them: the hydrostatic equation, continuity, the Bernoulli equation and the momentum equation — followed by the Reynolds number and the friction that separates a real pipeline from an ideal one.

It stops there, deliberately. Pumps and turbines, pipe networks, the Moody chart, open-channel flow, dimensional analysis and boundary-layer theory all belong to the courses that come after this one, and to Irrigation Engineering in year two in particular. Everything you meet here is something that course will assume you already know.

Scope of these notes

The syllabus follows Sleigh, An Introduction to Fluid Mechanics (CIVE 1400, School of Civil Engineering, University of Leeds), §0.7 to §4.3. The chapter table below gives the mapping section by section, so you can read either set of notes alongside the other. Worked examples and problems are written for agricultural and biosystems contexts rather than civil ones, but the theory is the same theory.

Learning Outcomes

On successful completion of this course a student will be able to:

  1. Work consistently in SI units, and check any equation for dimensional homogeneity before trusting the number it produces.
  2. Distinguish a fluid from a solid by its response to shear, apply Newton’s law of viscosity, and obtain density, specific weight, relative density and viscosity from tables.
  3. Determine the pressure at any point in a static fluid, express it as a head, and read a piezometer, a U-tube manometer and an inclined manometer.
  4. Compute the resultant force and the centre of pressure on a plane or curved submerged surface, both by the moments method and by the pressure-diagram method.
  5. Apply the continuity equation and the Bernoulli equation together to pipes, tanks, Venturi meters, orifices, notches and weirs — and state the conditions under which Bernoulli fails.
  6. Apply the momentum equation to find the force on a pipe bend, a nozzle, a flat plate and a curved vane, following a fixed six-step procedure.
  7. Classify a flow as laminar, transitional or turbulent from its Reynolds number, and compute the head loss for laminar flow in a pipe from the Hagen–Poiseuille equation.

Course Structure

Part I — Fluids at Rest

Chapters 1–2. Units and dimensions, what makes a fluid a fluid, the properties that describe it, hydrostatic pressure, manometry and forces on submerged surfaces.

Part II — Fluids in Motion

Chapters 3–4. Describing a flow, the control volume, and the three equations that follow from it: conservation of mass, of energy, and of momentum.

Part III — Real Fluids

Chapter 5. What changes once viscosity is admitted: laminar and turbulent flow, the Reynolds number, and head loss along a pipe.

Prerequisites

School-level physics and mathematics, and the first-semester engineering mathematics course. You need to be comfortable resolving forces and taking moments, evaluating a definite integral, and rearranging an algebraic equation. Differential equations are not required; the two that appear are separable and are integrated in the text.

Bring a calculator

Every chapter asks you to compute something, and the examples are worked through with real numbers rather than left as symbols. A scientific calculator is part of the equipment for this course, in class as well as in the examination.

On units

The notes are written in SI throughout, and Chapter 1 spends its first three sections on units and dimensions for a reason: mixing units is the single most common source of error in this subject, and it accounts for more lost marks than any misunderstanding of the physics. Where industry practice still quotes a value in another unit — head in metres but pipe diameter in inches, pressure in bar or in kgf/cm² — the conversion is given at the point of use.

Start here

Begin with Chapter 1: Fluid Properties. It is the shortest chapter in the course and the one every later chapter leans on.
